# The Liver and Protein Metabolism

Hello everyone. In today's third lesson we will talk about the liver and protein metabolism. We all know that protein is an important nutrient for the body, and most protein metabolism takes place in the liver. If liver function is damaged, protein metabolism goes wrong, and that sets off a series of health problems. So the liver plays an extremely important role in protein metabolism, and it is tied to many problems in the body.

Let me first talk about a decline in immunity. How does immunity decline? A decline in immunity begins with a decline in the liver's ability to make protein. Almost half of the body's protein is made in the liver. Plasma protein, for example, is made mainly in the liver. The amino acids absorbed from the digestive tract undergo protein synthesis, deamination, and transamination in the liver. In other words, the protein you eat is rearranged and reassembled into protein the body can absorb. The newly made protein then enters the blood circulation to supply the organs and tissues of the body. But when the liver is damaged and its function declines, its ability to make protein declines too. If not enough protein is made, it can directly reduce the body's ability to repair itself, which leads to a decline in overall immunity, including a weaker constitution and symptoms of sub-health. So when we notice our overall condition declining over a period of time, we should consider that the root cause may be a damaged liver.

Second, let us talk about purine metabolism. Abnormal purine metabolism is connected to a decline in the liver's ability to break things down. We know the main component of the cell nucleus is nucleic acid. What we call DNA, deoxyribonucleic acid, and RNA, ribonucleic acid, are its building blocks, and they determine our heredity, our ethnicity, and other inborn traits. So what is [[purine|嘌呤, a building block of DNA and RNA. When the body breaks purines down, uric acid is produced, which is why a purine-rich diet can raise uric acid levels.|https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/gout]]? Purine is a kind of substance produced during the constant metabolism of DNA and RNA themselves. Under the catalysis of enzymes, purine is further metabolized and broken down into the final product of protein metabolism, uric acid. Uric acid can maintain blood pressure, maintain mental sharpness, reduce oxidative stress on the blood vessels, and clear free radicals. But if purine metabolism is abnormal, it causes the uric acid level in the blood to rise, leading to [[hyperuricemia|高尿酸血症, a higher-than-normal level of uric acid in the blood. It can be symptomless but is the underlying condition that can lead to gout when uric acid crystals form in the joints.|https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/gout]]. So one of the reasons for high or low uric acid in the blood is the amount of purine synthesized in the body. Purine exists mainly in the form of purine nucleotides, and one of the ways it is produced is through the synthesis of free purine nucleotides in organs like the liver. In addition, because a large number of our body's cells rupture and die every day, when the liver's ability to break things down declines, its ability to metabolize nucleic acids also weakens, which eventually leads to abnormal purine metabolism and a series of problems.

In fact, the formation of disease in the body is a kind of butterfly effect. When liver damage reduces the ability to make and break down protein, the resulting decline in immunity, abnormal purine metabolism, and rising uric acid all promote more diseases, such as gout and kidney failure. How does gout form? Generally speaking, when the liver's ability to break down and metabolize declines, it causes abnormal purine metabolism and a rise in uric acid. The higher the uric acid, or the longer it stays high, the more easily gout develops.

When the uric acid concentration in the blood keeps rising, it precipitates locally as tiny, shapeless crystals. This is the mechanism behind [[gout|痛风, a painful form of inflammatory arthritis caused by uric acid crystals forming in a joint, often the big toe.|https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/gout]]. Because the blood vessels in the joint tissues are relatively few, and the joints are constantly moving, after exercise the joints are especially prone to oxygen deprivation and a buildup of lactic acid, which further increases the deposition of sodium urate. As more and more sodium urate builds up in the joints, the joints begin to be damaged. At that point the body automatically launches an immune response, releasing many white blood cells from the joint fluid to engulf these crystals. After engulfing them, the white blood cells rupture and release more proteases, or inflammatory factors, into the joint fluid. These enzymes and inflammatory factors then attract even more white blood cells to the joint, and they gather in ever greater numbers. The white blood cells keep rushing in to engulf the crystals, and after engulfing them they rupture, which produces still more enzymes and inflammatory factors. You can see how this vicious cycle keeps going, producing inflammation over and over again. This is how gout arises.

It can even lead to kidney failure. How does kidney failure form? The sodium urate crystals I just mentioned travel through the blood circulation to the whole body and enter the tissue fluid of various parts. Because the crystallized particles are large, they cannot enter the venous capillaries and be excreted through the systemic circulation. We know that anything that cannot enter the veins is very hard for the body to eliminate, so it deposits in the tissue fluid. Where does this deposition do the most harm? In the places richest in capillaries, such as the kidneys. When these uric acid crystals pass through the kidney, after being filtered by the glomeruli, ninety-eight percent are reabsorbed by the renal tubules. This increases the secretion and burden on the glomeruli, and over time it damages the renal tubules, including necrosis of the glomeruli. When the number of necrotic glomeruli reaches a certain level, it can easily cause uric acid nephritis. Imagine that as the uric acid crystals keep depositing, the damage worsens, and gradually the whole kidney dies. This is the kidney failure caused by uric acid. So to some degree, uric acid directly damages the kidneys, and this damage is irreversible. But when the deposition of uric acid causes the glomeruli to rupture, the body produces no obvious sensation at all. At this point the kidney is often already severely damaged, but you feel nothing. Because you feel nothing, it is easy to neglect, and you often miss the best window for improvement, which objectively worsens the kidney failure. In addition, as the uric acid crystals travel through the blood circulation, they can very easily cause high blood pressure, blood clots, and the destruction of pancreatic islet cells by the crystals, which raises the risk of diabetes.
