# Common Diseases That Trace Back to the Liver

Hello everyone. Today our topic is the relationship between several common diseases and the liver. Society is getting more developed, but at the same time all kinds of chronic diseases are becoming more common. Where do these chronic diseases come from? In fact, many diseases are closely tied to the secretion of hormones in our body. The human body has many hormones, and each plays a different role. For example, the pituitary gland secretes growth hormone, which promotes the body's development. The pineal gland secretes melatonin, which regulates the rhythm of life. The thyroid gland secretes thyroxine, which controls metabolism. The pancreatic islets secrete insulin, which controls blood sugar. These hormones are chemical substances secreted by the body's endocrine glands, and under the unified coordination of the nervous system, they play an important role in regulating the body's substance metabolism. We know that only a balanced hormone level does not harm the body. But today's unhealthy diet and the stress of life can easily make us take in extra hormones in daily life, which may cause disordered substance metabolism and affect the body. At times like this, an important organ of the body, the liver, comes into play. The liver turns certain hormones into substances that are inactive or less active, deactivating the excess hormones and lowering or removing their activity, so as to keep the body's hormone levels normal. The liver plays a decisive role in hormone metabolism. But if the liver's ability to regulate declines, then problems may appear in the process of deactivating hormones, and this triggers various diseases, such as hyperthyroidism, hypothyroidism, uterine fibroids, and autoimmune diseases, which harm our health.

First let us talk about hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism. The thyroid gland in our body is mainly responsible for secreting thyroxine, which controls the body's overall metabolism. It also adjusts the heat and energy the body produces, and it promotes digestion and the body's growth. When the thyroid's secretion is abnormal, the liver regulates it to keep the body's thyroxine in balance. But if the liver is damaged, its ability to regulate hormones declines, and the thyroxine in our body may become abnormal. When thyroxine is over-secreted and the liver cannot deactivate it, a person easily becomes nervous, restless, and anxious, with increased appetite but poor digestion, insomnia, weight loss, protruding eyes, eyelid swelling, and reduced vision, among other conditions. If it goes on for a long time, it can even trigger hyperthyroid heart disease. Conversely, when thyroxine is under-secreted, it easily causes fatigue, drowsiness all day, cold hands and feet, reduced appetite, weight gain, and sensitivity to cold, while the heartbeat gradually slows. All these abnormal thyroxine conditions are tied to a decline in the liver's ability to regulate. It is the liver's decline that leaves it unable to regulate them. A decline in liver function may not always show up in a hospital checkup. The checkup report shows some indexes, and these indexes are only for reference. Sometimes even if the indexes are normal, it does not mean your liver function is normal. This is easy to understand. Does a good grade mean a student is good? Or does a child doing well on one final exam mean he is a good student? If your liver data shows normal on one day of testing, does that mean your liver function is normal? Sometimes you cannot draw an equal sign.

Now let us talk about [[uterine fibroids|子宫肌瘤, noncancerous growths made of muscle and tissue that develop in or on the wall of the uterus. They are very common in women of reproductive age and vary widely in size and number.|https://medlineplus.gov/uterinefibroids.html]]. Uterine fibroids are among the common tumors of the human body, and the incidence is quite high among women aged thirty to fifty. How do they form? They mainly form when the smooth muscle cells of the uterus proliferate out of control. The incidence is very high, and the tumors vary greatly in size. The small ones can be under one centimeter in diameter, about the size of a soybean, while the large ones can reach ten kilograms, far exceeding the weight of a full-term baby. The number also varies, and there can be hundreds of them, which is called multiple fibroids. The resulting irregular menstruation, abdominal masses, pressure symptoms, pain, and even infertility cause a lot of physical and mental harm to women. Medical research holds that the growth of uterine fibroids is closely related to estrogen. Most of these fibroids occur after puberty and gradually shrink after menopause. Fibroids often enlarge during pregnancy or with the use of estrogen. In patients, the level of estrogen in the blood keeps rising. If estrogen is secreted abnormally and excessively, in the short term it causes thickening of the endometrium, and in the long term it causes uterine fibroids. Estrogen is also called the female hormone. It is a major female hormone that promotes the maturation of the female reproductive organs and the appearance of secondary sex characteristics, and it maintains normal reproductive function. Where is this hormone secreted? It is mainly secreted by the follicular cells of the ovary, and it is deactivated and converted in the liver, then combined with glucuronic acid and excreted in the urine. What does estrogen affect? It affects a woman's appearance and intelligence, and so on. When the liver's ability to deactivate estrogen declines, it may cause a woman's estrogen to become disordered. If the body's estrogen level is too high, the risk of gynecological disease and even breast cancer rises. If estrogen secretion is too low, it may bring the risk of menopausal syndrome, uterine atrophy, and even osteoporosis, coronary heart disease, and Alzheimer's disease, that is, senile dementia. So we say the liver plays a vital role in keeping the body's hormone levels stable.

The failure to deactivate estrogen caused by liver damage has a series of effects on the body, and especially triggers autoimmune diseases. This is a key point. To do both, the immune system first needs the ability to tell friend from foe, to distinguish the substances that benefit the body's health from those that harm it.
