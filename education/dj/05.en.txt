# The Liver Controls Your Blood Sugar

Hello everyone. Today we will talk about the liver's influence on blood sugar. The control of blood sugar highs and lows lies in the liver. Hearing the word blood sugar always makes us a little wary, because a spike can mean your body may be heading for big trouble. But in our body there is actually a precise blood sugar regulator, and that is the liver. It is the key organ that keeps blood sugar constant. It makes sure every mouthful of sugar we eat does not turn into a sweet burden. But these functions only reach their full effect when the liver is healthy. Once the liver is damaged, its role in regulating blood sugar is greatly reduced, and over time our blood sugar metabolism easily goes wrong, and sub-health and disease sweep in. So only a healthy liver becomes a strong guardian of normal blood sugar. Today let us talk about the liver's role in regulating blood sugar.

The liver's regulation of blood sugar is like a current account at a bank, where you can deposit and withdraw freely. When the blood sugar concentration in the body rises, the liver converts the blood sugar into liver glycogen for storage. When the body feels hungry or tired and blood sugar runs low, the liver breaks the glycogen back down into glucose and releases it into the blood to keep blood sugar at a normal level. So in the process of balancing blood sugar, the liver plays a decisive role. The liver contains many enzymes specific to sugar metabolism. Many metabolic processes, such as the synthesis and breakdown of glycogen, and the conversion of non-sugar substances into glucose, are called [[gluconeogenesis|糖异生, the process by which the liver makes new glucose from non-carbohydrate sources such as amino acids and lactate, especially between meals and during fasting.|https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/tests-diagnosis]]. That term is a bit technical, gluconeogenesis, which means turning non-sugar substances into glucose. These processes all take place inside the liver cells. Here we should add a word about why maintaining the liver is so important.

As you know, in mammals the liver is the main organ of gluconeogenesis. Under normal conditions, the kidney's gluconeogenesis ability is only ten percent of the liver's. What is gluconeogenesis? It is turning non-sugar substances into glucose. If your liver is not healthy, the kidney takes over this job from the liver. A few decades ago, during the years of natural disaster in China, a great many people developed kidney problems. Why? Because of a lack of nutrition, the liver was damaged first. After it was damaged, the liver's gluconeogenesis declined. In plain words, it stopped working. When the liver stops doing this job, who takes over? The kidney. So the kidney steps forward to do the job. But the kidney's ability in this area is only ten percent of the liver's, so it cannot handle the work, and the kidney's burden is greatly increased. So maintaining the liver is actually a way of caring for your kidneys. When the body needs it, through the action of nerves and hormones, the activity of the various sugar-metabolizing enzymes in the liver cells changes, so as to keep blood sugar constant. So the liver is the key organ that keeps blood sugar constant, and when the liver is damaged, it affects sugar metabolism and causes blood sugar to fluctuate, most commonly high blood sugar. Very often, because we lack a correct understanding of blood sugar, we turn pale at the word sugar. As soon as we see blood sugar out of the normal range, we easily think it is diabetes. In fact, at this point, as long as you regulate the damaged liver well, blood sugar can still return to normal, because although diabetes is marked by high blood sugar, high blood sugar does not necessarily mean diabetes. But if you have already been diagnosed with diabetes, that is, it is confirmed, then protecting the liver should be taken very seriously.

Why does having diabetes require protecting the liver? Because diabetes is a disease of disordered metabolism of sugar, protein, fat, water, and electrolytes caused by an absolute or relative deficiency of insulin secretion, together with a reduced sensitivity of the tissue cells to insulin. It is marked by high blood sugar, but it is actually a collective disorder of many substances, including sugar, protein, fat, water, and electrolytes. We know the liver is one of the organs insulin acts on, and the liver's regulation of blood sugar depends on the level of insulin in the blood and on its sensitivity to insulin. After we eat, blood sugar rises, and insulin sends signals to the cells throughout the body. These cells take up glucose and burn it directly as energy. But liver cells are different. They store glucose in the form of glycogen. When blood sugar drops, the insulin level drops with it, and the liver receives the signal and releases the stored glucose back into the blood, so that blood sugar stays normal between meals and overnight. But the liver cannot directly detect the rise and fall of blood sugar. Who does that work? Insulin passes the message. But when insulin is deficient or the liver is damaged, the liver's sensitivity to insulin drops, and it may misread the signal. Whether or not you have eaten, it keeps releasing glucose into the blood, which raises blood sugar. This is also why a diabetic patient may still have high blood sugar at night even though he has not eaten. In addition, because the liver can also process fat, when the liver is full of stored glycogen, it converts the excess glucose in the blood into fatty acids and stores them as fat as a long-term energy reserve. The particles containing fatty acids produced by the liver are carried through the blood to the whole body, and apart from a small part used by the body, most are stored in the fat tissue. Some of these fat particles are stored in the liver itself as fat, and when the amount reaches a certain level, what forms? It can form [[fatty liver|脂肪肝, also called non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, the buildup of excess fat in liver cells. It is most common in people who are overweight or have diabetes, and it can progress to liver damage.|https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/liver-disease/cirrhosis]], also called non-alcoholic liver damage. The great majority of people in this group are obese or diabetic. So diabetes and liver disease are risk factors for each other, and protecting the liver also greatly helps control blood sugar, including a balanced diet, enough nutrients, and exercise, which are active health interventions.

All in all, the liver plays an extremely critical role in controlling blood sugar. It is called the center of the body's sugar metabolism and the great sugar warehouse that keeps blood sugar steady. A person with a normal liver can automatically regulate and control blood sugar. But if the liver is damaged, or the enzymes responsible for metabolism in the liver lose their activity and become insensitive to blood sugar, then the balance between glucose and glycogen is broken, and danger appears. Abnormal metabolism follows, and then a series of problems. In the early stage it is high blood sugar, a symptom, not yet absolute diabetes. But if you do not pay attention and the liver keeps being damaged, the later stage is diabetes. So to improve the body's blood sugar metabolism, we should not only act on insulin and blood sugar, but should effectively regulate and activate the liver. Regulate and activate what? Regulate and activate the enzymes related to sugar metabolism in the liver. Only in this way can the body's great sugar warehouse, the liver, run normally and keep the body free of the sweet burden, truly adding points to health. That is all for today.
