# Getting to Know Your Liver

Hello everyone. Starting today, we will study one lesson a day in this course on liver health management. Today, let us begin by getting to know your liver. Our society is developing fast, and so is the pace of our lives. While we enjoy all kinds of modern conveniences, our health problems are also piling up. An unhealthy diet and a disordered daily routine directly affect the health of the most important organ in our body, the liver. So where is the liver? The liver sits on the right side of the abdominal cavity. It is the largest internal organ in the human body, and it plays a vital role in our health. For that reason, understanding and protecting the liver is one of the most important parts of looking after our health. If you press gently on the area of your liver and feel a slight pain, you may want to be alert to the possibility that your liver is already overburdened. That could already mean [[liver fibrosis|肝纤维化, the buildup of scar tissue in the liver that happens when the liver is repeatedly injured. Over time it can progress to cirrhosis.|https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/liver-disease/cirrhosis]], cirrhosis, fatty liver, or even a more serious liver disease, and it would be time to regulate and care for the liver properly.

As the most important organ in our body, the liver has many roles. It is not only the organ mainly responsible for metabolism. It also handles antioxidation, the storage of liver glycogen, protein synthesis, urea synthesis, and more. It is also the largest digestive gland in the human digestive system. Let me walk through the liver's main functions one by one.

The first is its metabolic role. The liver has an important relationship with protein metabolism. It is the main site where plasma proteins are made. Because plasma proteins drive the renewal of proteins in the tissues throughout the body, the liver's role in making plasma proteins matters a great deal for keeping the body's protein metabolism going. The amino acids absorbed from the digestive tract are used in the liver for protein synthesis, deamination, transamination, and so on. The newly made proteins then enter the blood circulation to meet the needs of organs and tissues throughout the body, or to repair them. So when the liver's ability to make protein declines, the body's ability to repair itself and its immunity will certainly decline as well.

The liver is also an important site of fat metabolism. It takes part in the digestion, absorption, breakdown, synthesis, and transport of lipids, and it is one of the main organs for making fatty acids, cholesterol, and phospholipids. When the liver is healthy, its cells automatically regulate the levels of cholesterol and [[triglycerides|甘油三酯, a type of fat in the blood. High levels are linked to a greater risk of heart disease and stroke.|https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/002404.htm]] in the blood. But when the liver has a problem, fat metabolism can become disordered, and fat can build up inside the liver to form a fatty liver. So when we develop obesity, high blood lipids, or high cholesterol, the first thing we should pay attention to is not rushing to change the symptom, but regulating our liver.

The liver is also connected to sugar metabolism. The rice and noodles we eat are broken down into simple sugars in the body. After being absorbed through the lining of the small intestine, they travel to the portal vein and then to the liver, where they are converted into [[liver glycogen|肝糖原, the form in which the liver stores glucose. When blood sugar drops, the liver breaks glycogen back down into glucose and releases it into the blood.|https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/tests-diagnosis]] and stored. Liver glycogen plays an important role in regulating blood sugar concentration and keeping blood sugar stable. When blood sugar rises, the liver converts it into glycogen for storage. When heavy labor or hunger causes a big drop in blood sugar, the liver cells break the glycogen back down into glucose and release it into the blood to keep blood sugar at a normal level. So if a person develops high blood sugar, it may already mean the liver has been damaged and its ability to regulate blood sugar has weakened.

The liver also plays an important role in hormone metabolism, and hormones are tied to regulation throughout the whole body. For example, the thyroid gland secretes thyroxine to regulate metabolism, the pituitary gland secretes melatonin to regulate sleep, and the adrenal glands secrete adrenaline to regulate the body's response to stress. Under normal conditions, the various hormones in the blood are kept at certain levels, and any excess is processed by the liver and deactivated, which keeps hormone levels stable in the body. But once liver metabolism goes wrong, the hormone levels in the blood become abnormal, and that leads to all kinds of problems. If estrogen is not deactivated properly, for instance, it can cause irregular menstruation and thickening of the uterine lining in women. If aldosterone and antidiuretic hormone are not deactivated, they cause sodium and water to be retained in the body, leading to edema and abnormal weight gain. This also helps explain something: why can a person who stays up late fail to lose weight? Some people object, saying, "I do gain weight when I stay up late, but as soon as I cut back, the weight drops, doesn't it?" The point is that if your liver is not healthy, what you are losing when you diet is water and protein, not fat. So a healthy liver also means the body can keep its hormone levels normal.

Second, the liver has a detoxifying role. It is the body's main detox organ, protecting the body from all kinds of harm. In the course of metabolism, the portal vein collects blood flowing back from the abdominal cavity. The harmful substances, microorganisms, and antigenic substances in that blood are processed by the liver, and toxic substances are turned into things that are harmless or far less toxic, then dissolved and excreted from the body. If the liver's ability to break things down declines, the toxins entering the body cannot be fully broken down or eliminated. The toxins that cannot be broken down may enter the blood circulation and create allergens, making the body prone to allergic symptoms. So when allergic symptoms appear, we should be on alert. It may be connected to a decline in the liver's detoxifying ability, and what you should do is regulate the liver promptly, not just suppress the allergic symptom.

Third, the liver has many other roles. For example, it has a synthesizing role. Besides storing liver glycogen and making protein, the liver is the main site for making several [[clotting factors|凝血因子, the proteins in blood that work together to stop bleeding by forming a clot. The liver produces most of them.|https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/000071.htm]]. Of the twelve clotting factors in the human body, four are made in the liver. The liver also promotes the secretion of bile. Where is bile produced? Bile is produced in the liver. Bile is a digestive fluid that helps absorb fats and fat-soluble vitamins. The bile produced by the liver is transported to the gallbladder for storage, and after being concentrated there it is released into the duodenum. The liver also has an immune defense role. The liver is rich in phagocytic cells that can engulf and clear foreign matter from the blood, so it is a major part of the body's immune defense system. The liver also has a blood-making function. In the embryonic stage the liver makes blood. A normal adult liver no longer takes part in blood-making, but it still retains the potential to do so, and under certain disease conditions it can regain some blood-making function.

All in all, the liver's functions are extremely important and closely tied to our health. Almost every moment, many complex biochemical reactions are taking place inside the liver. It is estimated that more than five hundred biochemical reactions occur in the liver every day. So when the liver is healthy, the body is healthy, and when the liver is not, disease will come looking for you. Only by protecting the liver in our daily life, through our lifestyle and personal habits, can we make a real, positive difference to our health. So now, have you come to know this most important organ in your body, the liver? See you tomorrow.
