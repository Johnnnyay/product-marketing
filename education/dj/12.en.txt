# Is Your Gut Healthy?

Hello, friends. In the last lesson we talked about how all disease begins in the gut, and at the end of the lesson we asked everyone a question: is your gut healthy? Some friends may say that there are no obvious symptoms at the moment, so they do not know whether their gut is healthy. If you are not sure whether your gut is healthy, please answer this question as best you can. First, did your mother use antibiotics or steroid hormones such as prednisone during pregnancy? Second, were you born by cesarean section? Were you breastfed or fed formula? Did you often have ear and throat infections as a child? Are you taking medicine that suppresses stomach acid? Do you often have allergic reactions, such as allergies to foods like gluten and milk? Do you often have diarrhea or constipation? Do you have type 2 diabetes? Are you overweight? Have you been diagnosed with an autoimmune disease?

If you do not know the answers, it does not matter. Let us see whether you have the following symptoms. First, stomach discomfort, such as excess gas, bloating, constipation, diarrhea, heartburn, and acid reflux. Second, changes in hair and nails, dull hair, and brittle, hard nails. Third, unexpected weight changes, a sudden rise or fall in weight over a short time without changing your diet and exercise habits. Fourth, sleep disturbance or persistent fatigue, such as frequent insomnia and tiredness. Fifth, skin irritation, such as a tendency to acne, rosacea, and eczema. Sixth, autoimmune disease, such as body-wide inflammation and disordered immune function. Seventh, food intolerance symptoms, such as bloating, diarrhea, abdominal pain, nausea, and food allergy. Eighth, bad breath, a mouth that easily has an odor. Ninth, mood changes, such as emotional instability. Many people have some of these symptoms, and some may ask why they appear.

A very important reason for these symptoms is that your gut has leaked. You may ask again, how can the gut leak? Because the structure of our gut has four major barriers. The first barrier is the flora barrier, formed by our probiotics mixed with some substances. The second layer is made of substances secreted by the flora, called the chemical barrier. Then there is the immune barrier, and the lowest layer is the mechanical barrier. These barriers protect the gut's toxins from entering our body, and the most important of the four barriers is the flora barrier. If the flora become imbalanced, it causes the chemical barrier to become imbalanced. When the chemical barrier is imbalanced, there are fewer probiotics and more harmful bacteria. The harmful bacteria then come into contact with our immunity and produce all kinds of inflammatory factors, causing the permeability of the mechanical barrier to increase. The professional term is [[leaky gut|肠漏, increased intestinal permeability. The idea is that the intestinal lining develops gaps that let partly digested food, toxins and microbes pass into the tissue beneath, provoking inflammation.|https://www.health.harvard.edu/blog/leaky-gut-what-is-it-and-what-does-it-mean-for-you-2017092212451]], which means the gut has leaked. After the gut leaks, the gut's toxins flow through the whole body. If the gut's toxins pass through the barrier and reach the brain, the brain will become inflamed, easily causing migraine, bad mood, and insomnia at night. Long-term inflammation causes the shrinkage of nerve cells, easily leading to depression, Parkinson's disease, and senile dementia, especially Alzheimer's disease. So for many brain problems today, you must first regulate the gut, and only then will the brain problems get better. The professional term is the [[gut-brain axis|肠脑轴, the two-way communication between the gut and the brain, involving nerves, hormones, and the gut microbiome. It is why gut health is increasingly linked to mood and brain conditions.|https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3337124/]].

When the gut leaks, many toxins pass through the portal vein to the liver, increasing the liver's burden. As you know, the liver is mainly responsible for detoxification and metabolism, so when the liver's burden is heavy, metabolic problems come at once, and the liver's detoxifying function also declines. The result is, in fact, all gut problems, and in professional terms we call it the gut-liver axis. You see, is gut health not related to our whole body?

The three forces we face today, environment, food, and stress, throw our gut flora out of balance, and this produces the symptoms above. Once the gut has a health crisis, it triggers all kinds of health problems. The most common are constipation, diarrhea, enteritis, and ulcers, and in severe cases it can even induce bowel cancer. Of course, the gut's health also affects the body's metabolism of blood sugar and blood lipids, leading to diseases like diabetes, high blood pressure, and fatty liver. Some obesity problems may also be caused by gut health problems, including the unhealthy gut mentioned above, which can also cause brain inflammation, easily producing migraine and insomnia. Long-term inflammation also causes the shrinkage of nerve cells, easily leading to depression, Parkinson's disease, and senile dementia, especially Alzheimer's disease. Have these crises caused by gut health overturned your previous understanding? Did you not expect that brain diseases like depression and senile dementia are related to gut health? Did you not expect that cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, including high blood pressure and fatty liver, are also related to gut health?
