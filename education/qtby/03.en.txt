# Start Your Health Journey With a Personal Health Record

Good evening to every friend who loves health, loves life and loves learning. I am Li Ling, and tonight's topic is the road to health, and why it begins with a personal health record. In our last class, our teacher described very vividly the kind of car we all need. Do you remember? A car with a clear dashboard, the kind that gives you a deep sense of safety. Tonight the baton has passed to me, and we are going to talk about what it takes to start building your personal health record.

Before I go into the topic, I want to share my own path into health. This photo was taken when I had just graduated from medical school; that is me on the right. I graduated in 2001 and went to work at a [[top-tier hospital|三甲医院, a "Grade 3A" hospital, the highest level in mainland China's hospital grading system. These are large tertiary hospitals that handle the most complex cases and also teach and do research.]] in Dongguan. At first I worked mainly in the outpatient department, and in 2004 I moved into clinical work in the neurology intensive care unit. Later I was sent to Huashan Hospital in Shanghai for advanced training in cerebrovascular intensive care. Over my ten years of clinical work, two things pushed me to change.

The first came from my patients. The longer I worked, the clearer it became that cerebrovascular disease and the conditions around it cannot be fully cured by medical treatment. We can only control the symptoms. After a first stroke, most people go on to have a second and a third, each one worse than the last, and almost all of them need medication for life. The age at which people fall ill keeps getting younger, too. The youngest stroke patient I treated was 22. That sense of powerlessness sat deep in my heart and pushed me to change. That was the first thing.

The second was me. Here is a picture of my work at the time. From the day I entered clinical work in 2004, staying up all night was routine. And here is my chest CT. Do you see the name on it? That is mine. I went from being a medical worker to being a patient. In our line of work we were constantly handling emergencies and resuscitations, so the pressure was enormous, our attention had to be total, and our nerves were always stretched tight. I was diagnosed with [[bronchiectasis|支气管扩张, a long-term condition in which the airways of the lungs become permanently widened and scarred, so mucus builds up and the lungs become prone to repeated infection.]] with a lung infection. Before that, I assumed antibiotics and anti-inflammatories could keep the infection under control, so I did not take it very seriously. Then, between 2012 and 2013, I coughed up blood twice. That was when I became frightened. I was already on the strongest class of antibiotics and they no longer worked; they could not control the infection. Coughing up blood only happens once the damage has built up enough to injure the blood vessels. At that point the option in front of me was surgery to remove the diseased part of the lung. But by then I had begun to learn about nutrition, and I knew that besides medical intervention there was another road I could choose: the road of health management through nutrition and cell repair.

For those two reasons, on September 1, 2013, I resigned from the hospital. Through my own changes and my own learning I have been building a health education presence of my own, and I hosted a health program on Dongguan radio for seven years. I want to bring more people onto the road of health management. My wish for these two years is to bring ten thousand families onto our learning platform. My own growth in health literacy took me from medical worker, to patient, to someone who promotes whole-body health, and I hope that change of roles gives you something to think about.

Of course, my change grew out of the core idea of our system's health management, which Teacher Jianjun introduced in our first class. Let's review it: when you are sick, see a doctor; your health depends on you; learn on the platform; manage across the whole life cycle; and you benefit by putting it into practice. Do not underestimate these five points. They look simple, but they hold a great deal of wisdom.

All five points run through the story I just told you. In the early days, when I had the lung infection, of course I used the hospital's methods, penicillin and anti-inflammatories, because the goal then was to control the infection. But as my condition got worse, I realized I was only controlling the infection and doing nothing about what was causing it: the late nights, the stress, the exhaustion. If those causes keep happening, toxins keep building up in the body, and the damage to my lungs would only get worse, and so would my illness. So I knew I could not go on like that. I had to rely on myself and take charge of my own health. I came to this system to learn while doing, to put it into practice, and to take action with [[clear, regulate, replenish and nourish|清调补养, the four-stage health management framework used throughout this course: clear toxins from the gut and blood, regulate lifestyle and the internal organs, replenish the nutrients cells are missing, and nourish the body in step with the seasons and the daily rhythm.]].

Once I acted, I benefited. That is what we mean by "you benefit by putting it into practice." I have not even told you yet what I gained. In the eleven years from 2013 to 2024, I have not taken a single dose of medicine, not had a single injection, and not coughed up blood again. Wouldn't you say my whole state of health has changed? Throughout all of this, I have held to the long view. Think about it: ten years, and the rest of my life ahead. I will keep managing my health in every dimension.

And when we talk about health management in every dimension, I am sure you remember what Teacher Jianjun gave us in his practical model of health management: clear, regulate, replenish, nourish. Our platform teachers will present all of this in much more detail, so be sure to follow our live classes.

Once we have the mindset, we need to put it into practice. The health chain has five links: prevention, health maintenance, diagnosis, treatment and rehabilitation. Diagnosis and treatment we hand to hospitals and doctors; I have marked those two in red. So what did I do after I left the hospital that changed my health so much? I adjusted my life. I stopped staying up late. I exercised. I worked on my emotions so my body would stop piling up toxins. I chose gut management and a liver care plan, used probiotics and plant nutrients in full amounts to clear toxins from my gut and blood, and gave my cells repair through complete nutrition. None of those steps had anything to do with diagnosis or treatment. All of them happened in prevention, health maintenance and rehabilitation.

Many people hold a misconception here. When they are ill, they know they need medicine, but they forget nutritional support. Doctors do remind us, when we are sick, that we must strengthen our nutrition. They simply do not have the time or energy to manage what we eat and drink, and they do not have time to do prevention, health maintenance and rehabilitation for themselves either. That is why so many medical workers have health problems, and why so many are in poor shape. It has been ten years since I left the hospital, and I know that several of my old colleagues have already been diagnosed with cancer. Medicine is a high-risk profession. Doctors put on the white coat and spend their own bodies to keep patients safe. It is hard and it is noble work, and we should fully recognize the value of doctors. Part of why I share all this today is that I hope my fellow medical workers can find a truly healthy road as well.

So, friends, prevention, health maintenance and rehabilitation run through our entire lives. Whether you are healthy, in the early stage of an illness, or ill, at every stage of life you need prevention, health maintenance and rehabilitation. Only when you are ill do you add diagnosis and treatment. Do you agree? I hope that as we go, you will keep strengthening your health thinking and understanding together with us.

Since we keep talking about health management, we need to understand what shapes our health. Look at this circle. It has four parts. What jumps out first? [[Medical care at 8 percent, genetics at 15 percent, and the natural and social environment at 17 percent|These shares, with lifestyle at 60 percent, are widely quoted in Chinese health education as a WHO estimate of what determines health. They are a teaching simplification rather than a precise measurement; the WHO's own discussion of the determinants of health stresses income, education, environment, relationships and behavior alongside access to care.|https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/determinants-of-health]]. Where do ordinary people, including my own friends and family, put their attention? On the 8 percent, medical care. The moment anything feels wrong, they run to the hospital, because in their minds the doctor is the last lifeline. Go to the hospital, get the tests, book a specialist. That is most people's reflex today. Seeing a doctor when you are sick is right, of course. But look: among the risk factors for health, medical care accounts for only 8 percent, and it is not something we control. It is passive health. Handing our health entirely to hospitals and doctors is, I think, a very bad bargain, and the losses to us, our families and our peace of mind can be enormous.

Doctors are under great pressure too. Their job is to resolve the symptoms of disease, using medical care to stabilize the condition and buy us enough time to do what? To repair. A doctor can resolve symptoms, but real recovery depends on the body's own power to heal: [[thirty percent treatment, seventy percent nourishment|三分治七分养, a common Chinese saying about recovery: treatment does part of the work, and the larger part comes from how you care for and nourish the body afterward.]]. That seventy percent is something we have to learn actively, and it lives in the 60 percent that is lifestyle, the part we can control. Yet so many people around us overlook it and never use that 60 percent for prevention, health maintenance and rehabilitation. I hope that from today, we pay attention to it together.

Since lifestyle accounts for 60 percent of our health, let's be clear about what a healthy lifestyle actually includes. I am sure many of you already know: an optimistic outlook, enough sleep, moderate exercise and a balanced diet. Everyone watching has heard and read this before. But let me ask: are you actually doing it? We all know how much these four habits matter, but our behavior does not follow. For example, it is already midnight. What are you doing? Scrolling videos on your phone. That disrupts the body's metabolism, detoxification and repair, doesn't it?

And when we do not live this way, what does the body do? Toxins build up, and inflammation rises. A great deal of inflammation is produced, immune cells are called out to fight, and the body is left in a state of high inflammation. In the end we also create [[hidden hunger|隐性饥饿, the WHO and FAO term for micronutrient deficiency: a person may eat enough calories, or too many, while still lacking the vitamins and minerals the body needs in small amounts.|https://www.who.int/health-topics/micronutrients]] in our cells and nutritional imbalance. I have just shown you this through my own story. The specifics of detoxification, fighting inflammation and correcting cellular hidden hunger will come in our upcoming live classes, one by one, so please stay with us.

I have talked about all these ideas because I want to keep strengthening your health management thinking. Now back to the personal health record. Teacher Liu Jin has already explained very vividly why building a health record matters. Tonight I am simply laying out the written points one, two, three as a reminder. First, we collect our information systematically and completely. Second, we come to understand ourselves fully. Third, it makes it easier to draw up a complete health management plan and to take stock of our health at any time.

What does a health record include? We covered it before: three tools, which I am sure you know well. What I want to stress is that we hope every one of you has a complete personal health record of your own, with all three tools in place.

Now a question: how do you see your [[physical exam report|体检报告, the report from a routine annual health checkup. In mainland China these are commonly done at hospitals or private checkup centers and include blood tests, imaging and basic measurements.]]? I ask because the exam report is what I will walk through with you next, and because I see two extreme attitudes among the friends and family around me. See which one you are. The first is fear and anxiety: being afraid of the checkup in case the report shows something serious. The second is going ten years without a checkup at all, because they do not take it seriously and simply avoid it. I believe both are wrong.

We should face the exam report with a positive attitude. We recommend that everyone have an exam report of their own every year, and read it from a health management point of view. Why? Go back to the five links of the health chain. Prevention, health maintenance and rehabilitation are the links that belong to health management. In our health education, I will walk through the report from that same point of view, so it is easier for you to put what you learn to use. If you do not have a recent report, I suggest going to a local hospital or checkup center and getting one done. Teacher Liu Jin from our last class is an example: her body sent her some signals, she got a checkup at the hospital, and from then on she paid attention to her health and managed it herself. Today, as you have seen, she is an excellent health manager.

So what should we focus on? Let me show you some numbers. The common, high-incidence conditions on this chart, each counted in the hundreds of millions, are what we need to watch: diabetes, high blood pressure, obesity, high blood lipids, and liver disease. In the next class I will pull these out and walk through them from a health management point of view.

Let me reinforce one more time what I just described as the root logic of health. An unhealthy lifestyle leads to the buildup of toxins, rising inflammation, and hidden hunger and nutritional imbalance in our cells. What causes toxins to build up, how to resolve inflammation, and how to repair cells with complete nutrition will each be presented by our teachers in the live classes ahead, so please make sure to follow them.

That is the end of my first class tonight. Thank you for watching. Let's keep using knowledge to guide our lives, and I look forward to seeing you next time. Thank you, everyone.
