Consider this: about 99 percent of the nutrients our body needs are absorbed through the gut, about 80 percent of toxins and metabolic waste must be processed by the gut flora before they can be eliminated, and [[close to 70 percent of our immunity comes from the gut|The figure that roughly 70 percent of the body's immune cells reside in the gut is a widely repeated estimate. The famous Greek physician Hippocrates, the father of modern medicine, was the first to say, as early as the third century BC, that [[all disease begins in the gut|万病始于肠, a principle attributed to Hippocrates and long echoed in traditional Chinese medicine. Modern research on the gut microbiome supports the idea that an imbalance of gut bacteria can drive inflammation linked to allergy, inflammatory bowel disease, obesity, diabetes and other conditions.|https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4425030/]]. In the nineteenth century the Russian biologist [[Metchnikoff|梅契尼科夫, Élie (Ilya) Metchnikoff, the Russian biologist who shared the 1908 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for his work on immunity. When the gut flora fall out of balance, toxins from the gut enter the body and set off a chain of systemic inflammation, and the permeability of the intestine increases. The technical term is [[leaky gut|肠漏, increased intestinal permeability. The idea is that the intestinal lining develops gaps that let partly digested food, toxins and microbes pass into the tissue beneath, provoking inflammation. Harvard Health notes it is an area of active research, with limited firm human evidence linking it to disease outside the gut.|https://www.health.harvard.edu/blog/leaky-gut-what-is-it-and-what-does-it-mean-for-you-2017092212451]].
