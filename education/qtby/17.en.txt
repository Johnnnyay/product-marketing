Today I take the baton from Teacher Li Ling to talk about another extremely important organ, the liver. There are some figures I feel I really must share with you first. Our country is a nation of liver disease. Fatty liver caused by an unhealthy way of living now accounts for fifty percent of all liver disease, and the total number of people with liver disease nationwide exceeds [[four hundred million|These figures are as the speaker presented them. For comparison, a 2014 review in the journal Hepatology estimated that around 300 million people in China had liver disease and that China accounted for about half of the world's deaths from liver cancer, so the scale the speaker describes is consistent with published estimates.|https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4867229/]], which means that among every three or four people, one has a liver problem of some degree. Globally, our country accounts for 45.3 percent of liver disease incidence and 46.6 percent of liver cancer incidence. Numbers like these are genuinely worrying.

Any one of us could carelessly become part of those statistics, so I hope today's lesson helps you take the health of your liver more seriously. Chinese medicine calls the liver [[the general|将军之官, the general among the organs, a description from the Yellow Emperor's Inner Classic. In Chinese medicine each organ is given a court title that describes its role, and the liver is the general because it plans, defends and keeps the body's qi moving smoothly.|]]. It earned that title because it truly shoulders an enormous amount of work and responsibility in the body. It holds off the body's enemies single-handedly, and at the same time it manufactures and trains a great many capable soldiers. Its role in the normal running of the body cannot be replaced.

So today let us first understand the liver from two angles, modern Western medicine and traditional Chinese medicine. From the picture you can see how Western medicine summarizes the liver's functions: the conversion of protein, fat, carbohydrate and other nutrients inside the body, the breakdown of harmful substances, the production of bile, the storage and redistribution of every nutrient, and the synthesis and regulation of a whole range of hormones. All of this is done by this one organ.

You could say that almost every internal organ depends on the liver to function properly. The Chinese medicine summary is a little more abstract and the vocabulary a little dry, but that is all right. If we connect it to what the body shows us in daily life, it becomes easy to understand. In spring the body's every function needs to wake up and become active, and that work is done by the liver. That is why spring is the season when liver care matters most. But the liver's workload really is heavy, so it deserves care in all four seasons.

The fibroids, nodules and hyperplasia that appear in our bodies, the thyroid problems that are increasingly common in modern society, women's uterine problems and breast problems, even the most ordinary shoulder and back ache and headaches, all trace back to the liver's job of keeping things flowing freely. Are there not also many people who lie in bed at night unable to sleep, tossing and turning like a pancake on a griddle? Others have a dull, gray complexion with no glow. Some young women have irregular cycles and cold hands and feet. All of these tell us that the liver's job of storing blood is not being done well. We can also judge how well the liver is working by looking at the nails, whether there are dark lines or pronounced vertical ridges, and at the eyes, whether they are bloodshot, dry, blurry or nearsighted.

I suspect that some of you in front of the screen are looking at your fingernails right now. There are also people who twist an ankle while walking perfectly normally, or who throw out their back without any big movement, or who get a cramp with no warning at all. That is not carelessness. It is liver blood running short, so that the fascia is too stiff and its flexibility far from ideal. Everyone now talks about learning to manage emotions, and it really is worth learning. Losing your temper or stewing in silent anger whenever something goes wrong truly harms both other people and yourself.

Notice how anger is followed by a headache, and how some people get so angry they cannot eat. That is liver qi stagnation, and it shows that the liver, spleen and stomach are not working together. If that emotional pattern is constant, it does continuous damage to the liver, and habitual or stubborn headaches are a very likely result. Examples are not hard to find.

We all know someone who is a so-called gallbladder-free hero. Twenty or thirty years ago this was fairly rare; now it is more and more common. In reality it is liver dysfunction that leads to gallbladder disease. Many people think nothing of having the gallbladder removed, but the gallbladder really matters. Removing it seems to solve the immediate pain, but many after-effects follow, such as duodenal ulcers, heartburn and reflux esophagitis. More troubling still, some years later the great majority of patients develop [[abnormal blood sugar|The speaker's point that gallbladder removal has metabolic consequences is supported by research. A 2018 review in the European Journal of Internal Medicine found that after cholecystectomy, the loss of the gallbladder's rhythmic bile release alters bile acid signaling and is associated with weight gain, disturbed glucose control, higher triglycerides and fatty liver.|https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8118133/]] and even diabetes, while the liver itself is affected, leading to high cholesterol, moderate to severe fatty liver, and even hepatitis and cirrhosis.

If you want to avoid that whole chain of problems, please take care of your liver. Having understood what the liver does, some friends will want to know what the body shows us in daily life, before any clinical disease appears, to warn us that the liver has been harmed.
