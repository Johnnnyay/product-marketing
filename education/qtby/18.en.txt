In fact the kidney in Chinese medicine and the kidney in Western medicine are not the same concept. In Western medicine, the kidney refers only to the organ, which excretes toxins and metabolic waste and regulates water, electrolytes and acid-base balance.

In Chinese medicine, the kidney is a whole system. The kidney is the [[root of life|先天之本, literally the foundation of what is congenital, or the root of one's inherited constitution. In Chinese medicine the kidney stores jing, the essence inherited from one's parents that governs growth, development, reproduction and aging, while the spleen and stomach are the acquired root that replenishes it from food.|]]. Its main functions include storing essence, governing growth, development and reproduction, governing water, receiving qi, governing the bones, producing marrow, connecting to the brain, opening into the ears, and controlling the two excretions. Do these terms sound obscure and hard to follow? I am neither a Chinese-medicine nor a Western-medicine doctor, and I am not here to teach medicine. What I want to explore with you is how to read the distress signals the body sends every day to judge whether the kidneys are working properly.

For example, many people today are very concerned about their children's growth and development, especially height, and infertility is also a very common problem. The core of both issues lies in the kidney. One of the kidney's most important functions is to store essence and govern growth and development. To solve these problems at the root, we have to nourish the kidney. The kidney is the root given to us by our parents. We are born with a fixed amount. If that inheritance is generous, we still cannot take it lightly; we still have to care for the kidney. If the inheritance is thin, then what was not given at birth has to be made up afterwards.

What we make up afterwards is the refined essence of food and water, which is what we ordinarily call nutrients. If the acquired supply cannot keep up, the kidney is depleted even faster.

So to nourish the kidney, first strengthen the spleen and stomach and protect the liver, because the liver and spleen generate each other, and when the liver is poor the spleen and stomach cannot be good either. Many people find their eyes puffy when they get up in the morning, and sometimes the lower legs swell. When the kidney fails at this, the mild result is swelling; the serious result is diabetes and cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease.

The classic picture of diabetes is three excesses and one deficit, and one of the excesses is excess urine. Too much urine carries the body's sugar and protein out with it. The core of the solution is still to strengthen the spleen and stomach and protect the liver first, and then nourish the kidney. Then there are people who are often constipated or have diarrhea, and even after gut management their bowel movements have not improved much.

The kidney regulates both urination and defecation physiologically, which is why Chinese medicine says it controls the two excretions. But the kidney has to work together with the liver and the gut to keep waste moving out of the body, which is what Chinese medicine means by the liver and kidney sharing one source. So we have to regulate liver and kidney together to fully resolve constipation and diarrhea.

Some people develop osteoporosis too early, lose teeth, have poor hearing, have hair that is dry and brittle like straw, go gray at the temples, or have thinning hair. All of these are signs of insufficient kidney essence.
