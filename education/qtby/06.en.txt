Let's go straight into part one. Ready? Assessing your obesity. As everyone knows, obesity has become a major disease of modern life and the root of every chronic illness. So how do we judge whether we are obese? Let's look at four indicators and see whether you have a handle on each. The first is [[body mass index|体重指数, BMI, calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. The WHO defines overweight as a BMI of 25 or above and obesity as 30 or above; the ranges the speaker gives are the tighter cutoffs used for Chinese adults.|https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/obesity-and-overweight]], one of the most widely used standards for obesity in the world. Everyone can calculate it, and the formula is simple: weight divided by height squared. Friends online, you can watch me do it, or calculate along with me.

Take me as an example. I weigh 70 kilograms and stand 1.79 meters. So 70 divided by 1.79, divided by 1.79 again, equals 21.84. Then find where you fall. The normal BMI for an adult man is 18.5 to 24, and for a woman 18.5 to 23. Did anyone get knocked out at the first hurdle? The second indicator is body fat percentage, also called body fat rate. It reflects the proportion of fat in the body. For adults, the healthy range for women is 20 to 28 percent and for men 12 to 20 percent. The higher the body fat percentage, the greater the likelihood of chronic disease.

The third indicator is body shape, also called the waist-to-hip ratio. Look at the figures below. If a man's waist circumference is over 85 centimeters, or a woman's over 80 centimeters, you already have central obesity.

Next measure your hip circumference. Divide waist by hip. If the ratio is above 0.9 for a man or above 0.85 for a woman, then your [[waist-to-hip ratio|腰臀比, waist circumference divided by hip circumference. A WHO expert consultation reviewed waist circumference and waist-to-hip ratio as predictors of chronic disease risk and proposed cutoffs for public health action.|https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241501491]] is over the limit, and that is central obesity too.

So right now, friends online, let's all put a hand on our belly and see whether there is a round bulge there. I notice that many people around me do not look fat, yet they have a little belly. That is actually the case that deserves more attention, because the fat on our limbs is mostly subcutaneous, while the fat in the belly is mostly [[visceral fat|内脏脂肪, fat stored deep in the abdomen around the organs. Harvard Health notes that visceral fat produces more inflammatory proteins than the fat under the skin, and that a larger waist is linked to higher cardiovascular risk even in otherwise healthy people.|https://www.health.harvard.edu/staying-healthy/taking-aim-at-belly-fat]]. Too much visceral fat means that high blood pressure, high blood lipids, high blood sugar and high uric acid may be waiting for us not far ahead. There is an old saying: the longer the belt, the shorter the life. So from now on, pay extra attention to your belly.

The last of the four is the visceral fat level. We have already covered why visceral fat is harmful. What I want to emphasize here is that the healthy range for visceral fat level is 4 to 6. Many people say, "Mine is only a little high, I am only 8 or 9, still within range." It does not matter; remember this sentence: the fat you cannot see is the more dangerous fat. The fat you cannot see is the more dangerous fat. When our visceral fat level starts to rise, we have effectively opened the door to chronic disease.

Let's review the four indicators for assessing obesity. Friends, are yours all healthy? One, body mass index: weight divided by height squared, then find your range. Two, body fat percentage: for men, 15 to 18 percent is healthy, and for women 20 to 23 percent is comfortable. Three, body shape: remember, the longer the belt, the shorter the life, so a man's waist should not exceed 85 centimeters and a woman's should not exceed 80. And four, visceral fat level: remember that the fat you cannot see is the more dangerous fat, so take the initiative, manage your health, and keep the visceral fat level between 4 and 6.

What exactly makes two people of the same age so different? One very important reason is the state of their muscle. Why do people become unsteady on their feet and look old as they age? Because our muscle keeps wasting away. So if we want to keep a good level of vitality as we grow older, we have to focus on our muscle level.

For muscle level, we focus on two indicators. The first is basal metabolic rate, and the question is whether ours is on the low side. The second is skeletal muscle. The higher the skeletal muscle, the higher the basal metabolic rate tends to be. More and more studies and reports show that muscle mass is related to immunity: if muscle tissue drops by 10 percent, immune function falls noticeably. Muscle mass is also related to the incidence of chronic diseases such as diabetes; the lower the muscle mass, the higher the rate of chronic disease.

So did you know? Muscle is not only about looking good. It is one of the important indicators for assessing our health. Remember this saying: saving money is not as good as saving muscle. The better your muscle ratio, the healthier your body, and the better your quality of life will be when you are older.

What does a good muscle ratio look like? Look at the lower left picture. If your skeletal muscle percentage is lower than your body fat percentage, the profile forms a C shape, which is essentially sub-health. If your skeletal muscle ratio, the middle bar, is higher than both your weight and your body fat, the profile forms a D shape like the one at lower right. That is our target, the healthy and strong profile.

To build muscle, we need exercise plus adequate nutrition. Remember, it is three parts exercise and seven parts nutrition. That is the key point of part two.

Part three brings us to the last key indicator, bone density. Did you know that the World Health Organization ranks osteoporosis as the second most damaging disease to human health after cardiovascular disease, and that together with diabetes and cardiovascular disease it is counted among the three great killers of health in middle and old age? But should we only start paying attention to bone density once we reach middle or old age? Let me ask you: do you often feel low back pain, a sore back, neck pain or shoulder pain? All of these pains are connected to bone health. So the earlier we pay attention to bone density, the better.

Bone density testing looks mainly at two numbers, the [[T-score and the Z-score|T值 and Z值. The T-score compares your bone density with that of a healthy young adult of the same sex: -1.0 or higher is normal, between -1.0 and -2.5 is low bone mass, and -2.5 or lower is osteoporosis. The Z-score compares you with people of your own age, sex and build.|https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/bone-density-scan/]], both calculated automatically after the test. If your T-score is above minus 1, that is normal. If it is below minus 1, it indicates reduced bone mass. If it is below minus 2.5, it indicates osteoporosis. The Z-score is one of the reference markers for judging osteoporosis. A gentle reminder here: children under eighteen are not advised to do a bone density test at the Nutrilite testing room. So what do we focus on next?

At what value should we start to pay attention? Do we wait until the T-score reaches minus 2.5 and we are first told we have osteoporosis? By then it may be too late, wouldn't you agree? So from the moment the T-score falls below minus 1, when it signals reduced bone mass, we must take it seriously.

Bone density health is inseparable from one concept I especially want to share: the calcium bank. The human skeleton is like our calcium bank. Look at the picture on the right. From the moment we are born, as we grow, every bit of calcium we take in each month is deposited into this bank account. Around age twenty we can deposit quite a lot each month. After twenty, the rate of deposit slows.

By the time we reach thirty-five, we can basically no longer make deposits, and instead we withdraw from the account every day. So in our whole life, thirty-five is when our bone mass reaches its highest value, called [[peak bone mass|骨峰值, the maximum amount of bone tissue a person reaches in life. The US National Institutes of Health puts the peak for most people in the mid to late twenties, after which bone is lost faster than it is rebuilt; a higher peak lowers the risk of osteoporosis later.|https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/kids-and-their-bones]], the moment our calcium bank is richest. And here is the key point: the higher your peak bone mass, the lower your chance of osteoporosis in old age.

So calcium supplementation before thirty-five is like making deposits and building up savings, and calcium supplementation after thirty-five is like drawing on those savings as little as possible, not sitting there eating through the whole account. Now do you see why calcium supplementation has to be a long-term commitment?
