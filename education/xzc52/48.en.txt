# Lecithin: A Foundation Nutrient for Every Cell

Today we begin a new topic, and together we will learn about lecithin. First, I would like everyone to be very clear about where lecithin belongs. So what is its place? Lecithin is a foundation nutrient. It is not something only one group of people needs. It is a nutrient that everyone needs to take.

Why is lecithin a nutrient that everyone must have? We need to know what the membranes of all the cells in the body are made of. They are made of lecithin. The lecithin in the cell membrane makes up 40 to 50 percent of the weight of the whole cell.

What shape is lecithin? What does it look like? If we magnify it, it has a big head, and that head is [[choline|胆碱. The main compound in lecithin is phosphatidylcholine: a choline "head" attached to two fatty acid "tails." Harvard's School of Public Health notes that choline is also used to make acetylcholine, a messenger in memory and thinking.|https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/choline/]]. Below the head it has two legs, which are two unsaturated fatty acids. Picture it for a moment. Isn't it a rather cartoonish figure? That is the shape of a lecithin molecule.

Lecithin has a special feature. What are those two legs for? They are made especially for grabbing on to fat. They can dissolve fat into water, and so lecithin can carry away the excess fat in our bodies.

The role it plays in the body is like what happens at home after a meal. With all those greasy pans and oily bowls, don't we use dish soap to wash them? Why can dish soap carry the grease away? Because dish soap has the ability to mix oil into water. That is really the same thing lecithin does inside the human body.

The role lecithin plays in the body is to carry away our fats, bring them into water, dissolve them there, and then help clear them out. That is what it does, and that is lecithin's most essential role.

So if we sum it up, four major functions grow out of this role. First, it supports the health of the blood vessel system. We will go into that in the next episode; today we will just give an overview of the four functions. Second, it benefits the liver and gallbladder. Third, it benefits the skin, especially the facial skin we all care so much about. Fourth, it benefits the nervous system.

First, a brief word on blood vessel health. For all the nutrients in our body to be delivered in time to every one of our internal organs, one essential condition is our blood vessels. They are the transport routes. If you joined all the blood vessels in the body end to end, how long would they be? The total length would reach about [[150,000 kilometers|The speaker's figure. The number most often quoted in Western sources is roughly 100,000 kilometers, or about 60,000 miles, for all the blood vessels in an adult laid end to end. Either way, the point is that the network is enormous.|]].

That is a great length, but not a single millimeter of those 150,000 kilometers can be blocked. If it is, even a one-millimeter blockage has a major effect on our health.

Imagine the process by which a blood vessel goes from open to blocked. If we grade it, we can call the most open vessel grade 1 and a completely blocked vessel grade 5. Both of these are actually fairly rare. A person is unlikely to have perfectly open vessels, and just as unlikely to have vessels blocked all the way to grade 5.

Most of us have vessels at around grade 2 or 3. What is grade 3? Half blocked. That is grade 3. But what I want to say here is that even with 50 percent of a blood vessel blocked, you may not feel much at all. You might only notice that you easily feel tightness in the chest or get short of breath. And when you have these symptoms, what does it mean? It means your vessels are already close to 50 percent blocked. As for the heart attacks, strokes, and blood clots in the legs we hear about, do these only happen at grade 5? Not necessarily. They can already happen at grade 4.

So let us come back to what lecithin does. It can carry away the fat that has built up in the blood vessels, dissolve it, and help clear it out.

That is why lecithin plays a very important role in keeping our blood vessels healthy. It can lower the levels of triglycerides and cholesterol in the blood. So remember that lecithin does two main things for the blood vessels. First, it carries away the fat clinging to the vessel walls. Second, it brings down two measurements in the blood, triglycerides and total cholesterol. This relates to keeping the blood vessels young, and we will go into it in the next episode.

Next, the second function. We have to talk about what lecithin does for the liver and gallbladder, especially the liver. We know that if the body is short of lecithin, [[fatty liver develops very easily|Harvard's School of Public Health explains that phosphatidylcholine carries fats away from the liver, so a choline deficiency can cause the liver to store too much fat. It also notes that it is unclear whether dietary choline or supplements can treat existing nonalcoholic fatty liver disease.|https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/choline/]]. Let us look at the whole process. When a person eats fat, where does the blood take it first? To the liver. What happens in the liver? The liver reassembles it into the kinds of fat the body needs, and then what? It has to send that fat back out of the liver through the blood to the whole body. That is the route by which fat passes from the liver into the rest of the body.

After the liver reassembles the fat, what does it need in order to ship it out of the liver? It needs a vehicle. This vehicle is dedicated to carrying fat, and no other vehicle can take its place. When a person is short of lecithin, it is as if that vehicle is missing. Can the fat still be shipped out of the liver smoothly? No, it cannot. So it slowly builds up, and the fat in the liver rises. That is the principle by which a lack of lecithin leads to fatty liver.

Next let us look at the gallbladder. Many people today have gallstones. I used to have gallstones myself, and through these nutritional methods they have gotten smaller and smaller. To be honest, the gallstones are still there, but they get smaller every year. The rate of gallstones in our country keeps climbing. What kind of person tends to get them?

Everyone knows that people who skip breakfast are prone to gallstones. Why? Because when you go without breakfast for a long time, the bile often does not flow. The cholesterol in the bile gradually becomes more and more concentrated, until finally it forms cholesterol crystals, and those become gallstones.

What can lower the level of cholesterol in our bodies, keep it from becoming more and more concentrated, and ideally let it flow away with the blood? [[Lecithin can help the cholesterol in the gallbladder dissolve better|In bile, phosphatidylcholine works with bile acids to hold cholesterol in solution, and cholesterol gallstones form when bile carries more cholesterol than these can keep dissolved. Whether lecithin supplements shrink existing gallstones has not been established.|]] into water and into the blood and carry it away, and that lowers the chance of gallstones.

Third is the skin, and this one is easy for everyone to understand. As we said earlier, lecithin is the main component of all our cell membranes. If we compare a skin cell to a grape, some people's faces are made of plump, juicy grapes, and others' are made of nothing but raisins.

You can picture it. The first looks fresh, bright, and moist; the second is completely rough. Why is it rough? Is it a lack of water? Lack of water makes skin rough. And what comes next? Peeling. Why does skin peel? Because the cells on the surface of the skin are damaged. Once they are damaged, the water in the cells evaporates, and doesn't the grape become a raisin? Then of course wrinkles appear more easily, and they are those deep crosswise lines that no skincare product can repair.

So the core principle here, as it relates to lecithin, is that lecithin protects the cell membrane. When your skin cells do not have these holes and the membranes are intact, water does not escape easily.

What standard can we use to judge the quality of our skin, and indirectly see whether we need lecithin? The first is the amount of pigment in the cells. If our cells are old and worn, pigment tends to build up in them, including [[lipofuscin|脂褐素, a yellow-brown pigment made of oxidized fats and proteins that builds up in aging cells. It is linked with the flat brown "age spots" the speaker mentions, although age spots on the skin (solar lentigines) are mainly caused by sun exposure.|]].

What is lipofuscin? It is what we usually call age spots. From age spots you can judge whether a person's cells are healthy, whether they are short of lecithin, whether they are short of water. So age spots are a sign of aging skin cells. What is the cause? A lack of lecithin to look after the membrane on the surface of the cell. The second thing to look at is how much water the cells hold. In one [[set of experiments|The speaker does not identify the study behind these figures, and I was not able to find it. Treat the 46.3 percent and 69.2 percent figures as the speaker's report.|]], with long-term lecithin supplementation, how much did lipofuscin go down? By 46.3 percent. At the same time the water content of the skin rose by 69.2 percent. That is a figure that says a great deal about what lecithin does.

Now if we look at what the same lecithin does in the brain, we need to talk about the human brain. The brain's function shows up mainly in memory. Memory is at its peak starting in early childhood, declines step by step after young adulthood, and in old age may fade. Memory, along with functions like attention, is also closely related to lecithin.

The reason is what we said earlier. The membranes of all cells are made of lecithin, and the membranes of nerve cells are made of lecithin too. What does a nerve cell look like? It has a big head at the front and a long, fiber-like tail trailing behind. What is that fiber? It is the nerve fiber in our body, and the membrane of the nerve fiber is also made of lecithin.

Here is an example. Suppose you are walking and someone suddenly says "stop." The leg you just lifted comes back down. At that moment your hearing is directing your limbs.

Here is another situation. When you are driving and see the light turn from green to red, you immediately step on the brake and stop the car. At that moment your visual signal is directing your leg. So there is a fiber from the hearing side that directs the leg, and there is a fiber from the vision side that also commands the leg. Both fibers direct the same leg. How are they wired? Just like the electrical wiring in a house, how do they run without the currents crossing?

In our homes, the water pipes and electrical lines have to be laid out very carefully, and nerves all the more so. The way signals travel along the nerve fibers in our bodies is just like the way current travels along the wires in a house. Several bundles of wire run together, and each wire has its own insulation. Our nerve cells are also wrapped in insulation on the outside, which ensures that the electrical signals commanding the same thigh muscles do not cross over or short-circuit. And what is the nerves' insulation made of?

It is made of lecithin. So who needs this [[insulation|The nerve's insulating sheath is called myelin. It is made largely of fats, including phosphatidylcholine, together with cholesterol and other lipids such as sphingomyelin. Myelin forms rapidly from late pregnancy through childhood, as the speaker describes.|]] the most? The group whose nervous system is growing and developing most intensely. It starts in the middle and later stages of pregnancy, when the brain is developing rapidly, and at that point the body needs lecithin as the raw material for the insulation. From birth all the way until the child starts school, the insulation keeps growing. A newborn's little hands stay clenched. A little older, the baby can bat at things. Older still, the child can pick something up alone but may not yet be able to use a pen. As the child grows, they can hold a pen and start scribbling on the walls and the floor. Later they learn to write, and when they grow up they might study art and learn to draw fine, detailed pictures.

The whole process I just described is a child's nerves steadily growing their insulation, so that the child can precisely control every fine movement of the body. Calligraphy and painting are both fine motor skills if anything is. Throughout this whole process, what is the raw material for the insulation? Lecithin.

Let us talk about the nerves some more. In our bodies, signals pass from one nerve to the next. What carries those signals? A couple of days ago our whole family climbed the Great Wall. What passes the signal between nerves? The signal fire between nerves is a derivative of choline. At the start we said that lecithin looks like a big head, and what is that big head? Choline.

So when we are short of lecithin, it means the body is short of the raw material for making nerve signals, and that will certainly affect how signals pass through our nerves. When nerve signaling goes wrong, a whole series of problems can develop in the nervous system. The most common is dementia. With dementia, 65 is the dividing line: before 65 it is called early-onset dementia, and after 65 it is called senile dementia. So those are the core functions of lecithin. Today we are giving an overview, and next time we will go into more depth. Since lecithin is so good, should we eat more of it in daily life, and what should we watch out for when we take it? You need to know the two things lecithin fears most. First, lecithin is very afraid of oxidation. It does not like contact with the oxygen in the air, because its two legs, as we said, are polyunsaturated fatty acids, and they oxidize very easily.

Second, lecithin is afraid of heat. Once it is heated, especially overheated, it becomes unstable, and if oxygen is present at the same time as the heat, even more of it is destroyed. How can we protect lecithin? The most scientific way is to dress it in a coat of vitamin E. That is why good lecithin always comes together with vitamin E.

Vitamin E is there to protect lecithin from oxidation, and that is what makes a lecithin high quality. You have to give lecithin a suit of armor. Without that armor, lecithin oxidizes the moment it goes into your mouth, because while you chew it, it comes into contact with the oxygen in the air very easily.

With vitamin E present, it can resist the oxidation of lecithin in the mouth. All right, today we have given an overview, and that is what I will share for now. In the next episode we will go deeper and talk about why lecithin can keep your blood vessels young. See you next time, and thank you.
