# Carotenoids and Vitamin A: The Eyes and the Linings

Today I want to focus on the carotenoids. What is carotene? It is the main source of vitamin A in the human body, and it acts as an antioxidant, regulates immunity, fights cancer and slows aging. Take lutein, one of this family. It is an antioxidant and it also filters light, so it protects eyesight, keeps vision from declining, and helps prevent cataracts. Let us start with lutein. [[Lutein is extracted from the marigold|叶黄素, taken from the flower 万寿菊, the African or Aztec marigold, Tagetes erecta, which is the main commercial source of lutein worldwide. Lutein and its partner zeaxanthin concentrate in the macula of the eye. In the National Eye Institute's AREDS2 trial, lutein and zeaxanthin replaced beta-carotene in the eye supplement formula, giving a small added benefit and avoiding beta-carotene's risk for smokers.|https://www.nei.nih.gov/research/clinical-trials/age-related-eye-disease-studies-aredsareds2/about-areds-and-areds2]], a very yellow chrysanthemum-like flower.

What does lutein actually do? Our eyes cannot manage without it. When you stare at something, there is always one area in your field of vision that is sharpest, in the middle, and the further out you go the blurrier it becomes. Sharp or blurry, the whole field you see is your retina, and the spot on the retina where you see most clearly is the macula. In plain language, the place your eye uses to focus is the macula. So what do we see with? Our eyes. And which part of the eye?

We see with the macula. And what is special about the macula? It can distinguish color. Notice how our vision gets blurrier toward the edges. That blurry region is your peripheral vision. Can you see things with peripheral vision? You can, but you will find it very hard to tell colors apart. Why? Because the cells that distinguish color do not grow at the edge of the retina. Where are they concentrated? Mainly in the macula. You can test this yourself. Fix your eyes on one point, then use your peripheral vision to look at someone beside you and ask yourself what color clothes they are wearing. You basically cannot tell. That is how the human eye works.

And if you do not pay attention to nourishing the macula, then as you get older it may develop [[macular degeneration|老年黄斑变性, age-related macular degeneration. The National Eye Institute describes the macula as the part of the eye that controls sharp, straight-ahead vision, and says that as the disease advances, blurry areas grow near the center of vision, colors look dimmer and straight lines start to look wavy or crooked. Risk rises after 55, and smoking and family history add to it.|https://www.nei.nih.gov/learn-about-eye-health/eye-conditions-and-diseases/age-related-macular-degeneration]]. What does that feel like? First, everything in front of you goes blurry, even in the macular region. Then colors become hard to tell apart, and gradually the image even distorts, so the whole picture changes.

So when we say that an older person's eyes have gone bad, and that an elderly relative no longer recognizes their own grandchildren, why is that? It may well be macular degeneration. The picture is distorted, the color is gone, and so they do not recognize people. It is a very painful disease for an older person.

Once macular degeneration sets in, it is extremely difficult to reverse. So what do you do? All you can do is prevent it and reduce the chance of it happening. And how do you prevent it? By taking lutein consistently. In this same family there is also [[astaxanthin|虾青素, a red carotenoid found in microalgae, and in the salmon, shrimp and crab that eat them. The speaker groups it under lutein; strictly they are two different carotenoids in the same broad family.|]], which has very strong antioxidant power and offers great help to the body in resisting inflammation and regulating immunity.

Now let us look at the carotenoids as a group. They include alpha-carotene, beta-carotene, zeaxanthin and the lutein I just described. Put them all together and what do you get? The carotenoids. It is one large class, and they share a common function, which is antioxidation. They are an antioxidant team. Nutritionally, the carotenoids are registered as having two functions: slowing aging and resisting mutation.

Besides those two, there is one more. [[Beta-carotene can be converted into vitamin A|MedlinePlus calls beta-carotene a provitamin A compound that also acts as an antioxidant. It notes that beta-carotene supplements do not reduce cancer risk and may increase it in smokers and people exposed to asbestos. Preformed vitamin A has an upper limit because large doses are toxic, and high doses in pregnancy can cause birth defects; the adult RDA is 900 micrograms for men and 700 for women.|https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/002400.htm]], so we can use beta-carotene as a safe source of vitamin A. Why should we take vitamin A? Because vitamin A is a vitamin that almost everyone today is short of. Why do I say that? Let us look first at what vitamin A does. Its first job is to maintain normal vision, especially the kind of vision we use in the dark, what we call night vision.

For our eyes to see, light has to enter the eye and set off a series of biochemical reactions. Once those reactions are complete, they are turned into an electrical signal that travels along the optic nerve to the brain, and only then do we register that we have seen something.

So do those biochemical reactions have to be quick and responsive? They certainly do. And any biochemical reaction consumes material, doesn't it? In particular, [[retinol and retinal|视黄醇 and 视黄醛, the alcohol and aldehyde forms of vitamin A. Retinal is the form that combines with a protein in the retina to make the pigment that responds to light, which is why MedlinePlus says vitamin A produces the pigments in the retina and why a deficiency causes night blindness.|https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/002400.htm]] are used up in large amounts, and these two are vitamin A. So the more you use your eyes, the more vitamin A you burn through.

Take a school-age child who writes and reads every day. Isn't that a heavy demand? And people today have a phone in their hand almost all the time, looking at a phone and a computer every day, and the blue light those screens emit burns through even more vitamin A. If we are only reading a book or looking at the scenery, in daylight, the consumption is relatively small. But the moment you look at a phone or a computer, you use several times more vitamin A than you would reading.

Because what are phones and computers? They are strong light sources. Your eyes have to maintain night vision, which means controlling the incoming light so it is not too strong, so your eyes and your retina are constantly in a state of adjustment. That is why looking at a phone or a computer uses up so much vitamin A.

Given how much we use today, if we were to get all our carotene from carrots, every person would need at least a kilogram of carrots a day. And if you play games all day, hour after hour of Honor of Kings, a kilo and a half to two and a half kilos would not be nearly enough. Can we eat that many carrots every day? Impossible. We are not rabbits. So people today never get enough vitamin A. Besides keeping the eyes normal, what else is vitamin A connected with? It maintains the health of the epithelial tissue cells and supports the synthesis of immunoglobulins, because vitamin A is involved in the formation and the maintenance of epithelial tissue.

A few days ago we said the B vitamins take part in the formation of epithelial tissue. What is the difference between B and A? The difference is that vitamin B leans toward forming the epithelial tissue, while A is mainly about keeping that tissue normal. The two work together. So when A is lacking, the epithelial tissue that has formed cannot maintain its normal structure, and what is it most likely to do? It [[keratinizes|角质化. Without enough vitamin A the surface cells of the skin and the linings of the body turn hard, dry and scaly. In the eye this is called xerophthalmia, and MedlinePlus notes that vitamin A deficiency can cause night blindness or permanent scarring of the cornea.|https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/002400.htm]], it turns hard and scaly.

For example, in dry winter weather some people get a great deal of dandruff, an unusual amount, coming off in flakes. What is going on? That is keratinization, and it tells us the person is already short of vitamin A.

When vitamin A is lacking, anywhere there is a lining, whether outer skin or the inner linings, keratinizes easily. Once that happens, the nose, the throat, the airways, the stomach and intestines, the urinary system and the reproductive system all keratinize more readily, and that keratinization weakens the natural barrier that keeps bacteria out.

Take the nose. What does it get? Rhinitis. So when you see people picking at their noses all the time, why is that? Because the mucous lining of the nasal cavity has keratinized. And once mucous tissue keratinizes, what follows? Resistance drops, and you get rhinitis, pharyngitis, laryngitis, tracheitis and bronchitis easily. What is the severe end of that? Pneumonia, meaning the lung too has keratinized to a degree, so its ability to resist bacteria has weakened and pneumonia sets in.

So whenever inflammation is long-standing, what does it mean? It certainly means a lack of vitamin A. If you do not replace it, the infections will keep coming back. Medicine may relieve it, but the moment you stop the medicine, the infection returns. This is especially typical in children, and we have seen it happen. Two children in a family infect each other in turn, coughing in turn, going to the hospital every month in turn. The medicine relieves it, and the moment they stop, it comes back. What is the reason? At the root, their nutrients are insufficient, which makes all kinds of infection very easy, and in children the infections keep recurring, with inflammation in the nose and throat.

The same logic applies in the stomach and intestines. If the stomach lining is short of A, it keratinizes easily. And where is that most likely to happen? At the part near the pylorus, the outlet of the stomach, because there is less stomach acid there, so bacteria are harder to kill off.

Once A is lacking, that spot keratinizes especially easily, and once it does, bacteria move in and have a place to grow. Bacteria feed on keratinized epithelial tissue, so you have handed them their food. Bacteria multiply there, and one in particular grows in exactly that spot. What is it called? [[Helicobacter pylori|幽门螺旋杆菌. MedlinePlus describes it as a bacterium that infects the stomach, often acquired in childhood, and says it is thought to spread through contaminated food and water or contact with an infected person's saliva. It is the main cause of peptic ulcers and can also cause gastritis and stomach cancer. Vitamin A status is not among the causes mainstream sources list.|https://medlineplus.gov/helicobacterpyloriinfections.html]]. It grows right there. So the moment you test positive for Helicobacter pylori, you are certainly short of A, because if your stomach lining were normal it could not grow there. And once you have it, is it contagious?

It certainly is. A family eating together every day passes it around very easily. I have heard of a family of six in which five had Helicobacter pylori and only one did not. The one who was not infected was the one who barely looked at a phone. The other five were all infected, and only the one with little screen time was not. What is the reason?

Because he looked at his phone less, he was not short of vitamin A. Now you can see it. So anyone infected with Helicobacter must be lacking vitamin A. If you are not short of A, the bacteria have nowhere to grow and nothing to eat, because there is no keratinized layer for them to feed on. The same reasoning holds in the stomach and in the intestines. Problems in the mucous tissue are all connected with this. So if you observe children who are short of A, you find infections very easily at both ends: above, infections of the respiratory tract, coughs and colds, and below, infections of the intestines, meaning diarrhea.

That is the whole picture a lack of vitamin A creates. What else does a lack of vitamin A affect? Crucially, it affects the reproductive system across the board, and that is an important subject. We will save it for next time. Thank you, everyone, and see you next time.
