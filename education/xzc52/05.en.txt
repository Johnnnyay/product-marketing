# China's Health Data, and the Limits of Medicine

Hello, everyone. This is our fifth lesson, and today we are going to look at China's health situation from a broader angle. Yesterday we said that if we do not study health, the problems get bigger and bigger and the holes get bigger and bigger. Today we will use China's big health data to see just how big the hole is. In 2013 there were 170 million people in China with high blood pressure. By 2018 there were already 240 million. What does that mean? It means one in four adults. As for [[diabetes, we are now close to 140 million people|A large national survey of 75,880 adults found that in 2015 to 2017 about 12.8 percent of Chinese adults had diabetes by American Diabetes Association criteria, an estimated 129.8 million people.|https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7186854/]], which is an extremely high proportion.

In 2013 one person was diagnosed with cancer every thirty seconds. Now it is one every seven seconds. And by now the proportions for diabetes and for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease are higher still. The highest death rate, of course, is still cancer, but if you add heart disease and stroke together, the two of them together come first overall.

In China, between 2017 and 2018, about 3.5 million people a year died of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease and about 3 million died of cancer. Those numbers are rising year by year, and rising fast. Faced with data like this, you realize that we are in the middle of it, so can we keep ourselves apart from it? It is hard to imagine. Think of anyone at random among the friends around you, and that person has high blood pressure, heart or brain vascular disease, or cancer. Are there a lot of cases like that? There are far too many.

Here is another question. From the year 2000 until now, twenty years, has our country's population been growing fast? The total population has not risen quickly at all. In fact you will find that many cities have declining populations. Big cities like Beijing and Shanghai are basically in negative growth. But from 2000 until now, has the growth of our hospitals been fast? Compare a hospital in 2000 with a hospital today and ask which is bigger. Aren't today's hospitals far bigger than before?

And there are far more of them than there used to be. So we discover that if medical treatment were the only road to health, hospitals would not keep getting bigger. The total population is roughly fixed, and against that fixed background hospitals keep getting bigger and more numerous. That by itself shows that relying on medical treatment alone will not give you complete health. That is the state of our hospitals now, and I believe we all feel it in much the same way.

There is another thing: illness is arriving younger. Many diseases are appearing in younger people, and the trend is getting clearer. We used to think we only needed to pay attention to health after forty or fifty. When do we need to start paying attention now? Right from birth. In the past a newborn would not catch a cold or run a fever until seven or eight months old, because the immunity the mother passed on could carry the baby for nearly half a year. Now you find newborns catching colds and running fevers before they are even a month old. What does that tell us? It tells us the mother had very little immunity to pass on, so she did not give the child any. That is a serious matter, because a baby that small cannot eat anything either, which makes it very difficult.

So when do we have to start paying attention to health now? Doesn't it have to start in the womb? That is, during pregnancy the mother should understand her own constitution, what she should and should not eat, why she should take plant protein, why breakfast is the first thing to improve, and how to give the child full and complete nutrition through breakfast. And if it is not there, how to supplement it. Without this knowledge, your child loses at the starting line. So it is not only from birth. In reality you should be attending to health while the child is still inside the mother.

Then there is this: between 2013 and 2014, among Chinese people aged thirty-five to forty-six, what proportion of deaths were from cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease? Twenty-two percent. That figure is already frighteningly high. And people born in the 1970s and 1980s have now become cancer's favorite targets, the main force of the cancer population. We used to think a person might get cancer in their seventies or eighties, but if someone really does get cancer at that age, the illness usually advances very slowly and death comes more slowly too, because in older people cancer progresses more slowly. In a young person, someone born in the 70s or 80s, cancer advances extremely fast. So young people today have become cancer's targets.

Now let us look at what many people say about hospitals, and this is not only about hospitals in China, it is the same everywhere in the world. One third of patients have problems that come from adverse drug reactions. One third is a high number. As for ordinary misdiagnosis, the figure I have here reaches 27 percent, and for serious illness the [[misdiagnosis rate reaches 40 percent|The speaker's figures. WHO's patient safety fact sheet gives different measures: medication-related harm affects about 1 in 30 patients in health care, and diagnostic errors occur in 5 to 20 percent of doctor and patient encounters.|https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/patient-safety]].

A rather unconventional British research institute has said that in certain people who do not understand health management, 80 percent of medicines are actually ineffective, and that the best intervention for a patient is less medical intervention. I heard of a friend who has high blood pressure. You tell him to take care of himself and he does not. All he has heard from other people is that if you rinse out your blood vessels once a year you will be fine, so why bother being careful, since high blood pressure cannot be cured anyway.

Then one Spring Festival he had been drinking, and after all the socializing he felt worn out, so he decided to find a hospital and get his blood vessels rinsed. Five bottles were dripped into him, and after that he went straight into liver and kidney failure. Within a week he was gone. Here was a man who had been perfectly all right, and a rinse took his life. In particular, some countries have explicitly banned the [[intravenous drips|The speaker is describing the Chinese practice of seasonal intravenous infusions intended to "rinse the blood vessels". Injectable products, including traditional herbal injections, carry a higher risk of severe allergic reaction than the same treatment taken by mouth.|]] now used for heart and vascular conditions, because the allergic reactions they cause are extremely severe. So one third of patients have problems from adverse drug reactions, and that is a worldwide statistic.

Here is another case. A man kept feeling foggy and heavy-headed while driving, so he went to the hospital for a checkup. The hospital's initial judgment was a cerebral hemorrhage. As soon as they gave him the medication he fell into a deep coma, and in the end he was moved from hospital to hospital with no result.

From going into the hospital, to the coma, to his death took three months. When the case was finally reviewed, it turned out the judgment had been wrong.

The doctor had diagnosed a cerebral hemorrhage, but what he actually had was a [[cerebral venous thrombosis|A clot in the veins that drain the brain. Its symptoms, headache and altered consciousness, overlap with those of a brain bleed, but the treatments pull in opposite directions, which is why distinguishing the two matters.|]]. Cerebral venous thrombosis is relatively hard to detect, because the symptoms are much the same as a hemorrhage. If it is a hemorrhage, the drugs used make the blood vessels contract, because contraction stops the bleeding. But with a venous thrombosis, you have to use drugs that dilate the vessels so the clot can pass through.

So with this misjudgment, what was really a cerebral venous thrombosis was diagnosed as a hemorrhage. Drugs that should have widened the vessels were replaced with drugs that narrowed them, the vessels contracted, the brain was starved of blood, and he went straight into a deep coma. That is how a misdiagnosis unfolds.

That is why the research institute I mentioned says that sometimes the best measure for a patient is less medical intervention. There was once a [[month-long doctors' strike in Colombia|A story widely repeated in health talks, that a strike by Colombian doctors was followed by a fall in the national death rate. We were not able to verify it in any original source, so treat it as an anecdote rather than as data.|]], and when the figures were tallied afterwards, the national death rate over that month had not risen at all. It had actually fallen somewhat.

So an American research institute formally published the view that when it comes to chronic noncommunicable disease, a highly developed medical system sometimes has very little to do with an individual's health.

I do not agree with that view, of course, but at the very least it shows that chronic disease needs something besides the medical system. It needs a person's own ability to manage their health.

Here is something else to understand: the clinical system and basic medical research are two different things. Basic medical research is of course the study of things that are good for human health, and most of it is carried out at universities, though some drug companies and pharmaceutical firms do this kind of research too. But what is the modern clinical medical system set up to do? The building of the modern clinical system is controlled by a few of the largest pharmaceutical companies in the world, so the first thing a clinical system built in the hands of those companies has to do is what? Make a profit. It has to earn a return, doesn't it? That is perfectly normal, because a company has to make money and has to have profits. It is not like a university doing basic theoretical research that does not have to consider profit because the state pays for it. Drug companies exist to make money. So remember that the modern medical system, in the United States just as much as anywhere else, and the building of the modern clinical system, first of all has to do what?

It has to make money. So facing this, our thinking has to change. It is all perfectly normal, since every industry in society needs to make money. But given this reality, we ourselves cannot be mere consumers of medical care. Use the medical system at the critical moment, but if you rely on it entirely in ordinary times, without the corresponding health management knowledge and ability of your own, it does not do much for your health. So our thinking has to change and we have to think this through, because anything that helps the medical system make money easily attracts investment, and anything that does not make the medical system money attracts investment and technical progress much more slowly.

So which hospital departments make the most money? Cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, oncology and orthopedics. Half a year ago a doctor at a top-tier hospital in Beijing told me face to face that orthopedics was the biggest earner at his hospital. And do these departments develop quickly? Extremely quickly, because every pharmaceutical company pours money into large amounts of scientific and technical research. Take surgery. Has it advanced quickly? Extremely quickly, and the technology is very advanced. What used to be major operations have become minor ones, and the techniques are very mature.

What about research into drugs for heart and vascular disease? Also very fast. And why is there so much research into them? Because once you start taking a drug for cardiovascular disease you take it for life. Many of them can never be stopped, so for safety's sake the companies have to keep researching, upgrading and bringing out new versions.

Is the market big? A market where people take something for life is enormous. Other drugs are not taken for life, so they improve comparatively slowly. Take colds, flu and infections. Once your cold is better, you stop taking the drug. With a bacterial infection, once the bacteria are gone and the infection has cleared, you stop, don't you? So the markets for drugs that are not taken for life are smaller, and they do not develop as fast.

Take the vaccines produced in China. Some of the technology we use to make them still dates from the 1960s. The rabies vaccine and the polio vaccine we are given use technology from when? The 1960s. Why has it advanced so slowly? Because you only need it once in a lifetime. Have any of you gone for a rabies shot recently? No. When do you go? Only when a dog has bitten you or a cat has scratched you. And once you have had the shot, do you need another? No. So with this kind of product there is no need for progress to be fast.

I am telling you all this so that you know what logic sits behind clinical medicine. Medicine has made enormous contributions to the health of humanity worldwide, and that is a fact nobody can deny. But is it enough for your health now? Clearly it is not. No matter how advanced medical care becomes, it is not enough on its own for a person's health.

Look at cancer. Is the development of cancer drugs fast? Extremely fast. New drugs come out every year. Are they expensive? Extremely. But do you use them? You have cancer, you are close to death, what is the use of holding onto your money? You use those drugs. If one tablet would definitely cure you but the drug cost a million yuan, would you use it? Many people would bankrupt themselves and borrow from friends to buy a cancer drug. So development is very fast indeed. And yet to this day there is still no drug that can wipe cancer out completely, even though the pace of development is fast every year and the money invested in it is enormous.

Another thing to understand is that clinical medicine treats symptoms directly. Look up the definition of clinical medicine in an online encyclopedia and you will see: the role of clinical medicine is to treat symptoms directly and eliminate them for you. A great many companies have poured a great deal of money into this, with the single aim of eliminating your symptoms. Of course, clearing symptoms is a big help in recovering from an illness. But does it do much for health itself? Not entirely, because many clinical drugs are useful for restoring your body to sixty out of a hundred. That is a pass, isn't it?

But for bringing your body back to eighty out of a hundred they are basically useless, because when you walk out of the hospital you do not instantly become a healthy person. Everyone understands that. So some unconventional American research institutes have shown that 30 to 40 percent of operations do not need to be performed at all. Is it the same in China? We do not know. We are only talking about American findings. And among the factors related to Americans' healthy lifespan, [[only 10 percent is connected with medical care|The speaker's figure is close to a widely cited estimate. Clinical care is generally credited with about 10 to 20 percent of modifiable health outcomes, with behavior and social and environmental conditions accounting for the rest. The remainder is not attributed specifically to nutrient intake.|https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8276667/]]. What is the remaining 90 percent connected with? With health management and with nutrient intake.

So from that number we can draw a lesson. Facing the same great enemy of chronic noncommunicable disease that all of humanity faces, for Americans only 10 percent has to do with medical care. What exactly are the solutions for the other 90 percent? That becomes the direction in which we progress and upgrade, doesn't it?

In China there is also the problem of birth defects. September 12, 2006, was our country's first Prevention of Birth Defects Day. When something becomes a commemorative day, what does that tell you? It tells you the problem is serious, and it already was. Why do we have Mother's Day? Because a lot of children do not respect their mothers, so we need a Mother's Day. Why do we need a Prevention of Birth Defects Day? Because the proportion of birth defects keeps rising. In 2006 a baby with a birth defect was born every thirty seconds.

And from 2006 until now, might that have doubled? Here is one more thing. We all feel our economy has improved and that the children at home surely do not lack for food. But the health data show that 80 percent of students have breakfasts of rather poor nutritional quality, that the rate of anemia in adolescence reaches 38 percent, and that the rate of nearsightedness is 32.5 percent among primary school students, 59.4 among junior high students, 77.3 among senior high students, and 80 percent among university students. China has 270 million students in school. Their protein intake is only 65 percent of the standard, they are seriously short of iron and calcium, and their [[vitamin A intake is only 15 percent of the standard|These are Chinese school health survey figures cited by the speaker, measured against China's own dietary reference intakes. We were not able to locate the original report.|]]. What is the reason for this? At a time when we all feel that eating is not a problem, how can students' nutrition be this poor? We will carry on with that tomorrow. Thank you, everyone.
