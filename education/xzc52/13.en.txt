# Sleep, Repair, and the Sleep Cycle

Today is lesson thirteen, and we are going to talk about sleep and its relationship to our health. Yesterday I mentioned that the [[2017 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine|Awarded to Jeffrey Hall, Michael Rosbash and Michael Young for discovering the molecular mechanism of the circadian rhythm, the roughly 24-hour internal clock that times sleep, hormone release and metabolism. Their work was on the clock itself rather than on sleep as such, which is why the speaker frames it as proof that the daily rhythm matters.|https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6138759/]] went to three Americans. What exactly did they study? They studied sleep, and the conclusion they arrived at was that sleep is very important. Look at that: they won a Nobel Prize for it. All of us already know sleep is important, so why did they get a Nobel Prize? Because they proved it. They showed why sleep is important, and that is what earned the prize.

Why is sleep so important? For years the media have run articles urging people not to stay up late, and yet when I look around, a great many people still do not take the harm of late nights to heart. So what is the harm, exactly? We know the earth rotates once every 24 hours, and within those 24 hours the light that living things receive changes on a fixed cycle. Almost every form of life on earth has adapted to this alternation of day and night by developing its own way of regulating its physiology and its behavior. Human beings are naturally no exception.

Put simply, your body has a mechanism that, at the right moment, sends a signal to each of your organs telling them it is time, get up. And what do the organs do once they are up? The most important thing is cellular metabolism. That is the job they wake up to do.

So if the body's day and night rhythm is thrown off, you can imagine what happens to the normal metabolism of your organs. It goes wrong, and after that illness follows. That is why we need enough sleep. The first reason is repair. Sleep puts the body into a state of repair, because during deep sleep growth hormone levels are high, and the body repairs itself easily. So the first reason is that the body's functions need repairing.

The second reason is clearing toxins. During the day, while we work, the running of our brain produces a great deal of waste, and the waste produced in the head especially is not broken down on the spot. Those toxins have not been dealt with, so they have to be cleared at night, and how? [[Through sleep|A 2013 study in Science showed that during sleep the space between brain cells expands by about 60 percent and cerebrospinal fluid flushes out metabolic waste, including beta-amyloid, far faster than while awake. This is the research behind the idea that the brain cleans itself at night.|https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3880190/]], which breaks them down.

Take an example. If you go one night without sleep, then two nights, you will certainly feel that your head is extremely heavy. Why so heavy? Because the toxins have built up, and with no sleep there is no way to clear them. Think about it: which does more harm to your body, going one or two days without sleep, or going one or two days without food? Two days without eating and you can still get by mentally. But two days without sleep and you will find that you are a completely different person. So sleep is very important, because it repairs and it detoxifies.

Among the factors that affect sleep, the first is your hormone levels. What is the hormone that controls falling asleep? [[Melatonin|A hormone released by the pineal gland after dark that signals the body it is time to sleep. Light, and blue light from screens in particular, suppresses it. Harvard researchers found that blue light suppressed melatonin for about twice as long as green light of equal brightness and shifted the body clock by twice as much.|https://www.health.harvard.edu/staying-healthy/blue-light-has-a-dark-side]]. Melatonin governs the onset of sleep. When it is time to sleep at night it is secreted, and what affects it most? Light.

So if there is a lot of light around you when you are about to sleep, less melatonin will be secreted. The blue light from phones and the blue light from computers have a particularly strong effect on melatonin. So as far as possible, do not look at your phone in the half hour before bed. Do not keep telling yourself that scrolling helps you fall asleep. Even if you do drift off with the phone in your hand, your deep sleep will be shorter. Melatonin matters at the other end of the night too. When you get up in the morning and the sun hits you, the melatonin breaks down, and that is why you are full of energy for the day. Without sunlight the melatonin does not fully break down, and you feel groggy and heavy all day. So melatonin is very important, and good living habits are the ones that do not interfere with its normal secretion. At night, sleep with the lights off as far as you can. Even after you have closed your eyes and fallen asleep, the light still affects your melatonin.

Over the long term, one result is poor sleep quality and a weak, depleted body. Another is that a small proportion of people develop [[insulin resistance|A state in which cells respond poorly to insulin, the step before type 2 diabetes. A 2022 Northwestern University study found that a single night of moderate room light, about 100 lux, during sleep raised next-morning insulin resistance in healthy adults. Studies linking long-term light at night to diabetes are observational, so the speaker's claim that sleeping with the lights on causes diabetes goes further than the evidence.|https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8944904/]], meaning the cells can no longer use insulin, and what does that lead to? Type 2 diabetes. So people who sleep with the lights on for years are prone to type 2 diabetes, the insulin-resistant kind.

So sleep with the lights off whenever you can. If you are traveling and staying in a hotel, turn on the small floor lamp, as long as it is not shining directly on you. That is how light affects sleep. There is one more thing about sleep we need to master, and that is the [[sleep cycle|睡眠周期. One pass through light sleep, deep sleep and REM sleep. Harvard Health puts the average cycle at about 90 minutes, with four to six cycles in a normal night.|https://www.health.harvard.edu/sleep/what-is-sleep-architecture]]. Many people say, I have to get six to eight hours a night. Is that right? Modern research shows that it is not really a question of how many hours you sleep. It is a question of completing each sleep cycle.

When we sleep, we move from light sleep into deep sleep, from deep sleep into the rapid eye movement stage, and finally to waking. That is one complete sleep cycle. How long is it? About an hour and a half. So when is the best time to wake? In the rapid eye movement stage, not in the middle of deep sleep. Someone who wakes out of deep sleep has a body that has not prepared itself to be awake, and a person who wakes at that point feels dreadfully tired.

If you sleep until you wake naturally, none of this matters; you wake when you wake. But if you get up at a fixed time, say I must be up at 7:00 every morning, then you need to work out your sleep cycles first. Suppose I sleep four cycles. How many hours is that? Six. Add the half hour it takes to fall asleep, and that is six and a half hours, so to be up at 7:00, when do I need to be asleep? By 12:30. And if you want one more cycle, push it back another hour and a half: be asleep by 11:00.

What if you cannot get to bed by 11:00? I recommend waiting until 12:30. Here is why. You have a fixed wake-up time. You missed 11:00 and fell asleep at 11:30 instead. You sleep through to 6:30, a cycle ends, and you wake, only to find the alarm has not gone off yet. So you think, I will sleep a little longer, and you drop straight back into deep sleep. Then at 7:00 the alarm rings and you are pulled out of deep sleep, and that is an exhausting way to start the day.

There is a book called [[Sleep|睡眠的革命 in Chinese: Sleep, by Nick Littlehales, a sleep coach who has worked with Manchester United and other clubs. He argues for counting sleep in 90-minute cycles rather than hours, which is the method the speaker is teaching here.]] that was written specifically for the European football clubs. Those players compete constantly, and their sleep has to be scientifically designed; sometimes they use the time on a plane to sleep exactly one full cycle. So this is what I mean by the sleep cycle.

The same logic applies to naps. If you sleep at midday, either sleep 20 minutes or, if you are going to go past half an hour, sleep a full cycle: half an hour to fall asleep plus a cycle of an hour and a half. So either 20 minutes or a full two hours. If you sleep one hour and then wake, you will usually spend the afternoon groggy and uncomfortable, and sometimes it would have been better not to nap at all. So complete the cycle.

Some people say, my work is busy, I cannot guarantee a fixed amount of sleep every night. What then? For friends in that situation, my advice is not to insist on six to eight hours every single night. It does not have to be that way.

Maybe tonight you get only three cycles, and tomorrow you can add one. But every night you must get at least two cycles; that is the minimum, two cycles a night. Over time it averages out: some nights more, some nights less, six to eight hours a day on average. If we sleep more than [[9.4 hours a day|These cutoffs are the speaker's. A 2010 meta-analysis of 16 studies and more than 1.3 million people found a U-shaped relationship: both short sleep and long sleep were associated with higher death rates from all causes, about 12 percent higher for short sleepers and 30 percent higher for long sleepers, compared with seven to eight hours.|https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2864873/]], nearly ten hours, or less than 3.6 hours, not even four, both extremes seriously affect how long you live. So do not sleep too little, and do not sleep too long. That is the role of sleep.

You can see that sleep is very important, and scientific sleep especially so. Pay attention to your sleep, above all your deep sleep. That was the second of the controllable factors, sleep.
