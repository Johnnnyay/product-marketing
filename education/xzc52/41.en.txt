# Dietary Fiber: The Overlooked Nutrient

Today we begin a short introduction to dietary fiber. Is dietary fiber important? Of course it is, very much so. With the progress of modern science, more and more experts are studying dietary fiber, and the more they study it, the more important they find it. In the past people did not take dietary fiber seriously and did not treat it as a nutrient at all. Chemically, dietary fiber has the same structure as a carbohydrate, except that the body does not absorb it, so for a long time nobody paid it much attention.

Today it gets more and more attention. When did that start? From the 1980s, in the United States, in Europe, and across Western countries. As we know, in those countries the poor and the rich do not live together. The poor live in slums and the rich live in wealthy neighborhoods, and at that time the rich and the poor also ate different food. The rich ate only refined white rice and white flour, while only the poor ate coarse whole grains. That pattern held all the way into the 1990s. And what did they find? It was the rich who had high blood pressure. And diabetes, who did it mostly strike? The rich.

So what did people call these problems? Diseases of affluence. The phrase "diseases of affluence" became popular starting in the 1990s.

The US FDA, the Food and Drug Administration, saw that something was wrong. So they took dietary fiber out on its own and made it a nutrient in its own right. They held that people need more dietary fiber in their diet every day. This happened in [[1998, when the FDA began to require|The speaker's date and wording. I could not verify an FDA action in 1998 matching this description. Fiber appeared on US Nutrition Facts labels under the 1990 labeling law, and the FDA adopted a formal definition of dietary fiber in 2016. The disease list is the speaker's; Harvard's School of Public Health describes the colorectal cancer evidence as mixed.|]] that fiber be taken in adequate amounts, as a nutrient that can effectively help prevent obesity, coronary heart disease, diabetes, and rectal and colon cancer.

Dietary fiber is not absorbed by the body, and yet we cannot do without it. So where does it do its work? The first thing dietary fiber does is increase the feeling of fullness, which helps a great deal with weight control. Every day we want to eat until we are full. Being full is a happy thing, isn't it?

But remember what is written in our genes. In primitive society, every meal had to be eaten until you were stuffed. Why? Because there might not be a next meal. Eating your fill at each meal was insurance against having nothing to eat at the next one. In other words, what does eating until you are full at this meal mean? It means eating more than you actually need. Eating to fullness already means you have gone past your real requirement.

Back then, people did it in case the next meal never came. But do modern people have that problem? When you finish this meal, is there a chance there will be nothing to eat at the next one? No.

That means if you eat until you are full at every meal, every day, then every meal is too much. Eating until full means eating too much, and anything in excess is of course not good. Too much carbohydrate or fat is harmful. In Chinese, the word for a mistake is literally "going past" plus "wrong": to overdo something is to be in the wrong. Eating too much is wrong. So eating your fill at every meal was not a mistake in the past, but it has become one now.

What did the ancients say about eating for health? How much should you eat at each meal? Until you are seventy or eighty percent full. But think about how that feels. If you stop at seventy or eighty percent at every meal, don't you feel you never quite get enough, as if you had hardly eaten? In our genes, being full is deeply satisfying. When you have eaten your fill, are you happy? Do you feel good?

Of course you are happy and content. When we are full, the body releases feel-good hormones. As soon as you are full, you feel happy. Why do people love buffets so much? Just hearing the word "buffet" makes you feel happy, doesn't it? We joke that at a buffet you walk in holding on to the wall and walk out holding on to the wall, and that is the highest level of achievement. And if after you have eaten your fill at the buffet you have an ice cream, a sense of well-being just wells up. Fullness produces a sense of happiness, so if you do not eat until you are full, the happiness is gone.

So the body wants those feel-good hormones. We want the feeling of fullness, but we cannot take in too much fat and carbohydrate. What do we do? Eat more dietary fiber before the meal. And it is the same once you sit down at the table: eat the vegetables and fruit first, then the carbohydrates.

A few years ago my wife and I met an expert, and ever since we have followed the scientific order of eating he recommended, which he called [["grain, fruit, vegetable, mushroom, bean, grain"|The speaker's words, 谷果蔬、菌、豆、谷, as recorded in the transcript. The exact sequence may have been garbled in transcription. His practical point, stated right after, is that vegetables come first to line the stomach.|]]. It came out of a large amount of nutrition testing done in China. The vegetables in particular must be eaten first to line the stomach, and after that it is fine to eat until you are full. So to increase fullness you need enough fiber. Fullness matters, because once you hold yourself back from eating your fill, your mood is off. At the very least your optimism is gone.

So fiber increases fullness while keeping you from taking in too much fat and carbohydrate. Dietary fiber also promotes bowel movements, helping prevent and relieve constipation, because if the gut is to move quickly, there must be enough dietary fiber.

When the gut moves quickly, the food we eat and the waste it leaves pass through the digestive tract quickly, so harmful things are less likely to breed, and in particular carcinogens are less likely to form.

Think about food in summer. If you leave it out from morning to evening, doesn't it go bad and start to smell? We put food into the large intestine, a place at 37 degrees Celsius with a hundred trillion bacteria in it. Won't it go bad? And once it goes bad, many carcinogens can easily form. So we must have enough dietary fiber to move food residue and waste through faster. This is especially true for us East Asians, because the intestines of East Asian people are longer than those of white people. Why? What are we?

We come from farming peoples who have always eaten mostly plant foods. And white people? They come from herding peoples, who have always eaten a lot of meat. In the animal kingdom, meat eaters have short intestines and plant eaters have long ones. So Westerners have relatively short intestines, and we East Asians have relatively long ones. [[East Asians generally have about 8 meters of intestine, and white people generally only about 5|The speaker's figures. I found no reliable source showing an ethnic difference of this size in human intestinal length. Measured intestinal length varies widely between individuals and also depends on how it is measured.|]]. See how big the difference is?

Three meters. So food passes through them much faster than through us, and for them it is not much of a problem. We, on the other hand, must have enough dietary fiber to keep the gut moving. This is why we say: with enough dietary fiber you will have fewer toxins, and without enough, more toxins naturally stay in the body.

In addition, the water-soluble kinds of dietary fiber form a film in your intestine that lies against the intestinal wall. Once that film forms, the sugar and cholesterol we absorb go in more slowly, because the film holds them back. This prevents blood sugar from shooting up after a meal and helps lower cholesterol. [[Soluble fiber|Harvard's School of Public Health describes soluble fiber as dissolving in water to form a gel that slows digestion, which can help lower blood glucose and blood cholesterol and reduce hunger. It recommends at least 25 to 35 grams of fiber a day for children and adults.|https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/carbohydrates/fiber/]] also improves the gut flora, improves the function of the digestive tract, and helps prevent appendicitis. That is dietary fiber.

To sum up, dietary fiber has three major functions. The first is as a regulator: it controls how quickly carbohydrate, fat, and cholesterol are absorbed. The second is as a cleanser: it promotes the movement of the gut and helps carry harmful substances out of the intestine.

The third is as a nourisher. In the colon, microbes break fiber down into a great deal of [[short-chain fatty acids|短链脂肪酸, mainly acetate, propionate, and butyrate, which gut bacteria produce by fermenting dietary fiber. They are an important energy source for the cells of the colon, and research is exploring how they may affect the brain, mood, and behavior through the gut-brain connection.|https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7005631/]], and these provide nourishment to the colon. Short-chain fatty acids are very important. You can look up the roles they play in fighting cancer, in supplying nutrients, and in human mood. When you have time, look them up on Baidu Baike, the Chinese online encyclopedia. Search for short-chain fatty acids and you will find what they do in the body, and you can read about how they influence the body's mood, cognition, and behavior.

So if you have enough dietary fiber, the beneficial bacteria in your body can easily break it down into short-chain fatty acids that nourish your body. Those are the three major functions of dietary fiber.

Having come this far, we can see that dietary fiber always works together with probiotics. What are probiotics? They are something the body already has. The body naturally contains a great many bacteria. As a fetus we have none, but as the mother nears delivery, her birth canal becomes covered with beneficial bacteria, so in a natural birth the probiotics are passed on to the next generation along the way. If the baby is [[born by cesarean section|Research on delivery mode found that babies born vaginally acquire bacterial communities resembling the mother's vaginal microbiota, dominated by Lactobacillus, while cesarean-born babies acquire communities resembling skin bacteria.|https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2900693/]], those probiotics are not passed on. That is the first route by which a mother passes probiotics to her child.

The second route is breastfeeding. So if a child has had neither, the child's flora will certainly be very thin, with few kinds and small numbers, and that affects the child from the very start.
