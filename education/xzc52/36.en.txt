All right, let us come back to vitamin A. A couple of days ago a friend asked me why a lack of vitamin A leads to night blindness. Today I will explain the principle again. We all remember the old film cameras. Ten or twenty years ago there were no digital cameras yet, and we all used film, didn't we? The film came in rolls, and after you finished shooting you had to take it to a special shop to be developed. Didn't that film have a layer of chemical on its surface, what is called a photosensitive material?

I remember once in university I went out on a photo shoot, and it taught me a huge lesson. I packed the rolls of film in my suitcase, and at the airport the luggage had to go through security. I forgot to take the film out. The moment it passed through the X-ray machine at security, every roll was exposed. I had not taken a single picture, and the film was ruined.

So we understand how old-fashioned film makes an image: depending on the light, the exposure differs, and that shows up as different colors and pictures. Our eyes work the same way, except that the back of the eye cannot change rolls the way a camera does. The back of our eye is a single piece of film used over and over. When our eyes take in light and images from the outside world, they are exposed roughly a hundred times a second. The back of the eye also has a layer of light-sensitive material, and every exposure uses it up. Before the next exposure, the eye forms a new layer of [[light-sensitive material|感光物质. In the retina this is rhodopsin, a pigment in the rod cells built from a protein and a form of vitamin A called 11-cis retinal. Light changes the shape of the retinal and bleaches the pigment, and the eye then regenerates it through the visual cycle. An inadequate supply of vitamin A to the eyes causes night blindness, the oldest eye disease on record.|https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3265843/]] as fast as it possibly can.

So the moment you see light, in a flash, that layer of light-sensitive material breaks down, and that is how you see the light. Then, before the next exposure, the back of your eye forms a new layer at a speed you can hardly imagine. Imagine how many times in a single second the eye has to form and break down that material. If you have plenty of vitamin A, you form this material faster than other people, so isn't your vision better than theirs? But if a person is short of vitamin A, synthesis of the light-sensitive material cannot keep up, so won't things easily look blurry? That is why we say that a person's eyesight is a question of their vitamin A metabolism.

The next question: vitamin A is so good, so how do we take it? How do we take it in safely? There are two ways to supplement vitamin A. The first is to take vitamin A directly. But we all know that vitamin A is fat-soluble, which means it dissolves in fat. If you take it long term and are careless about the amount, it is easy to become poisoned. Taking too much may do some damage to the body.
