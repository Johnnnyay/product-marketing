# The Factors We Cannot Control

Welcome to lesson eleven. Today we will look at the factors affecting our health that we cannot control. The World Health Organization tells us that if we think of health as 100 percent, then among the factors that influence it, [[the natural environment accounts for 7 percent|This breakdown (lifestyle 60, heredity 15, social conditions 10, medical care 8, environment 7) is widely attributed to the WHO in Chinese health education, but it does not appear in a WHO publication we could locate. Public health research does broadly agree that behavior and social conditions shape health more than medical care does.|]], medical care 8 percent, social conditions 10 percent, and heredity 15 percent. These are the uncontrollable factors. Then your lifestyle accounts for 60 percent. These are all the factors that affect our health. If we are going to manage our health, do we need to manage all of them? Of course we do. Let us take them one at a time. First, manage the part of the natural environment that concerns you: sunlight, air, and water, and also make sure the place you live is neither too cold nor too hot.

Look around the world: wherever the climate is extreme, you do not find long-lived populations. So sunlight matters. If you live year after year in a north-facing home that gets no sun, the cold and damp in your body, as Chinese medicine puts it, will certainly become very heavy. This is something to pay special attention to.

We all need to get more sun. Sunlight, air, and water are the most basic conditions for any living thing to survive. You cannot manage the air outside; if it is polluted, how could you? You cannot control the outdoor air, but can you manage the air in your home? You can, and there are standards for it. For example, a high-quality air purifier is something a home needs. Then there is the water we drink. How many glasses of water should we drink each day? Eight or more. If you drink that much water, does its quality matter? Of course it does. And we all know in our hearts what the water quality in our country is like, so a good, high-quality water purifier is also a must for every household. So sunlight, air, and water, the parts that concern us directly, should be adjusted as well as we possibly can. Although in theory they account for only 7 percent of health, in practice their effect on how long we live is very large. This is very important.

Another factor is medical care. Our medical conditions today are fairly good. In China, Western medicine is highly developed, and we should understand it. Western medicine raises our quality of life, especially in emergency care and rescue, where it does an excellent job. So in China today it is actually not that easy to die. If something happens, emergency treatment arrives quickly, and most people are not easily lost.

But now that chronic non-communicable diseases have become our number one enemy, it is also not easy in China to live in good health. So in China today it is not easy to die, and it is just as hard to live healthily. Given how hard that is, what should we do? We need some knowledge of our own. What is Chinese medicine, what is Western medicine? We should know a little about both. You cannot know nothing, because once you understand, it helps you make health decisions, and making the right health decisions is very important.

This is especially true in China, one of the few countries in the world where medicine and education have been commercialized on a large scale. We all understand that the more commercialized medicine and education become, the more important it is for us to make our own decisions. The logic is simple. The money is money we earned ourselves and money we spend ourselves, so we must make the decisions ourselves.

What does the commercialization of medicine mean? It means the state pays relatively little. And if the state pays little, where does the money come from? It comes out of our own pockets, right?

So on the medical side, if you take what the state spends on medical care each year per person, and look at medical spending as a share of GDP, [[we rank 145th in the world|The speaker's figure. For context, China's total health expenditure in 2018, public and private combined, was about 5.9 trillion yuan, or 6.57 percent of GDP, below the share typical of high-income countries.|https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7918561/]]. We rank even lower than some of the African countries we give aid to. After President Xi came to office he set out to improve this, and one of the methods used was [[separating medicine from drug sales|医药分离. Under China's zero-markup drug policy, public hospitals had to stop adding the 15 percent markup they previously charged on medicines; by September 2017 it applied to all public hospitals. Studies found hospital revenue shifted from medicines toward services, tests and medical materials.|https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8562510/]]. In the past, when the state did not fund hospitals, what did they do? They earned money by selling drugs. Now they can no longer profit from selling drugs, but the state has not made up the money either. So what then? Doctors still need to be paid, and the hospital still needs money to run. Where does it come from?

So look at what often happens now when you go to the hospital: they are more inclined to recommend surgery, because much of a hospital's income now comes from operations. Think about it. When we have surgery, our organs are safely taken out and put back in, and that is definitely a risky thing. Compared with ten-odd years ago, the number of people advised to have surgery has grown a great deal. This is a phenomenon we cannot change, and I do not mean anything more by raising it here than to tell us how important it is to make our own decisions.

If you are ever facing surgery, I hope you will consult several doctors, especially doctors who have no connection to you at all.

For a doctor, the plan he offers can differ depending on whether or not you are his patient. Can you understand that? Doctors are human too, and their mindset shifts. This has nothing to do with commercial interest. Whether you are in his hands or not, he may instinctively lean more conservative or more aggressive. That is a completely normal human response to a problem. If you are in his hands, he may be under performance review pressure. If you are not, his mindset is entirely different.

In addition, at certain hospitals, certain doctors are under pressure to hit performance targets. There was once a retired doctor who had worked in men's urology. Out of his own interest he developed a Chinese-medicine physiotherapy device specifically for treating prostatitis, and it passed the relevant national clinical certification. He then asked the hospital director whether the device could be used in the urology department. The director said, if it works, use it. After a year of use, the director called him in and asked whether the device was working well. He said it worked very well. The director said, it works well enough, but this year your department's share of drug revenue is poor and your department's income is poor. How are we going to pay bonuses to all the doctors in your department?

So the income was poor, and what happened? In the end the device had to be withdrawn. After it was withdrawn, the doctor fell into depression, because intellectuals often have a strong attachment to their ideals. So you can see that in certain places, a project that brings in no income gets dropped. This is not the doctor's problem, and it is not the director's problem. What kind of problem is it? It is a problem of the social situation, right?

That is why our own personal knowledge of medicine has become especially important. If you have no knowledge yourself, you may carelessly lose an organ without quite knowing how. You cannot blame the doctor, and you cannot blame the hospital. It is a mistake in your own decision, right?

So you can understand that doctors are in fact victims too, and hospitals are victims too. In this period of transition in medicine, should we not know a little more ourselves? In Western medicine, which conditions call for surgery, and which have not yet reached the threshold for it? Today there is Baidu Baike, and looking things up is quite convenient, right? Sometimes when a symptom appears, the guidance is to have it checked once a year. But very often, whenever the guidance is a yearly check, some hospitals will tell you to have surgery.

Because the logic at certain hospitals today is this: you will need surgery sooner or later, so sooner is better than later. Take cardiac stents. Abroad, [[an artery has to be more than 90 percent blocked before a stent|The speaker's figures. Stents hold open a narrowed artery; afterward patients take aspirin and other antiplatelet medicines, usually for a year or longer, to prevent clots. Anti-rejection drugs are used after organ transplants, not stents.|https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/stents]] is put in. In China, the standard is 70 percent. Going from 70 percent to 90 percent generally takes ten or twenty years, so you are having the stent put in twenty years early, and having a stent twenty years early means taking anti-rejection medicine twenty years early.

Why not spend those twenty years using Chinese medicine to rebalance the body, or using nutrients to rebalance it? We have said so much, and is it not all about who should take responsibility? Is it not we ourselves who must? And if we are to take responsibility, should we not learn, within reason, some basic knowledge of Western medicine and some basic knowledge of Chinese medicine? Do not just listen to others who say Chinese medicine is useless and does not work. First find out for yourself the fundamental difference between Chinese and Western medicine, when Chinese medicine works, and what it can actually help you achieve.

And what are nutrients for? What can they do that Chinese and Western medicine cannot? All of this helps you make your own decisions. That is the second of the uncontrollable factors, medical care, and our hope is that through learning we can make it a little more controllable. To make better decisions, you can talk with doctors at authoritative institutions, and not limit yourself to one or two doctors. Consult several authoritative doctors. More and more good doctors are now studying nutrition and studying health management, and that is a very good development.

The third uncontrollable factor is the social environment, by which we mean the family environment and the work environment. Some people's health problems are related to pressure at work, and some to relationships at home. Here is a case. A woman had uterine fibroids and took medication for a long time, but it simply did not work well. Later it emerged that her family relationships were very strained. Her relationship with her husband was extremely tense, and that was why her condition never improved; however it was treated, it would not get much better.

So do the family environment and the work environment matter? They matter a great deal, and they account for 10 percent. Then there is heredity. Many people misunderstand this. Friends often say, why do I have high blood pressure? Because my dad has high blood pressure and my mom has high blood pressure, so I have it too. We say high blood pressure is not inherited in that sense. It is not a genetic disease, so do not push the responsibility onto the generation before you. If there is a hereditary factor, it only makes the condition easier to trigger, and it has to be triggered by something outside. If nothing outside sets it off, it will not appear. And what sets it off has to do with your habits. That is different from a genetic disease.

A genetic disease is something like [[thalassemia|地中海贫血, literally "Mediterranean anemia". It is an inherited blood disorder in which the body does not make enough hemoglobin, leading to anemia. On the speaker's second example: according to the US National Library of Medicine, pattern hair loss involves many genetic and environmental factors and also affects millions of women, though women rarely go completely bald.|https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/thalassemia]], a disease in which the red blood cells do not mature properly. That is a genetic disease. Some men go bald, and that is genetic too, and baldness is passed to sons but not to daughters. If you have that gene, you will go bald sooner or later. That is a genetic disease: if you have the gene, it will definitely show up. A hereditary tendency is different. It needs an outside trigger before it appears. Most chronic non-communicable diseases are not genetic diseases. What are they, then? There may be a small hereditary component, but something from outside has to trigger it before the disease appears.

In other words, the bullet may be chambered, but if you do not pull the trigger, it will not fire, right? That is how to understand hereditary factors.

So if we do carry a hereditary factor, we have to make some changes to our lifestyle. Take smoking. Some people smoke and live past 100, and some people smoke and die of lung cancer in their fifties or sixties. That has to do with their inherited constitution. You cannot compare yourself with others and say, he smokes and lives to 100, so why can I not smoke? Your genes are not the same as his. You need to change. You need to quit. There are also people now who suggest you get genetic screening to find out which cancers you are at risk for and whether your cancer risk is high.

What we say is that if you have good habits, no bad vices, and above all understand health management, we would suggest you do not necessarily need screening. After screening someone tells you your risk of lung cancer is very high, and then you think every day that you are going to get lung cancer. Even without lung cancer, you could think yourself into it. That is not the right way to handle it. Sometimes when the plan that follows screening is wrong, it causes problems of its own. For example, there is a famous American actress, [[Angelina Jolie|The actress carries a harmful BRCA1 variant; her mother died of ovarian cancer. She had a preventive double mastectomy in 2013 and had her ovaries and fallopian tubes removed in 2015. According to the US National Cancer Institute, more than 60 percent of women with such variants develop breast cancer, versus about 13 percent of women overall, and risk-reducing surgery substantially lowers that risk; removing the ovaries brings early menopause.|https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/genetics/brca-fact-sheet]], who starred in Tomb Raider. She had genetic screening, and the doctor told her that her risk of breast cancer was very high. Her grandmother had died of breast cancer, and her mother had also had breast cancer. She herself had nothing wrong at all, but the doctor told her the chance of her getting breast cancer was very high. If you heard that, what would you think? Would you be afraid?

Absolutely afraid, absolutely terrified. So in the end, out of fear, what did she do? To avoid breast cancer, she had both breasts removed.

In 2015 Jolie announced that, out of worry about ovarian cancer, she had also had her ovaries and fallopian tubes removed. What kind of life does Jolie live now? She has to take large doses of hormones regularly to keep her estrogen at a normal level; otherwise she would develop masculine features and endocrine disorders. In the United States, breast cancer is the most common cancer among women, yet the United States has announced that [[breast cancer incidence has been falling|The speaker's claim. According to the US National Cancer Institute, the rate of new female breast cancer cases in the US has been rising by about 0.7 percent a year (2014 to 2023), while the breast cancer death rate has been falling by about 1.2 percent a year.|https://seer.cancer.gov/statfacts/html/breast.html]] in recent years. Why?

Because many breasts that had not developed cancer were removed, so of course the rate is falling. It is not because the environment got better. It is because a great many healthy breasts are gone. By that logic, for lung cancer you remove the lungs, and for liver cancer you remove the liver. So if you have an inherited predisposition and you do not adjust your lifestyle, then even with the breasts removed, your constitution is still one that forms tumors, and nothing has fundamentally changed. So this is really a question of lifestyle. How much of our health does lifestyle account for? We need to know: 60 percent. What we covered before, including heredity, the environment, the family, and society, are together called the uncontrollable factors.

But there are also factors we can hold in our own hands, called the controllable factors, and that is lifestyle. How much does it account for? 60 percent. It includes an optimistic outlook, enough sleep, appropriate exercise, and a sensible diet with good nutrition. In other words, we have no way to take hold of the uncontrollable factors, but can we improve them somewhat?

How do we improve them? By adjusting our own lifestyle. That is why a healthy lifestyle is so important. It includes the four points I just listed. In China they are called the four cornerstones of health, and internationally they come from the [[Victoria Declaration|维多利亚宣言, the Victoria Declaration on Heart Health, issued in 1992 by the Advisory Board of the International Heart Health Conference held in Victoria, Canada. In China its message is taught as the "four cornerstones of health", usually given as a balanced diet, moderate exercise, no smoking and limited alcohol, and psychological balance.|https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1336655/]]. You can look up the Victoria Declaration yourself. It puts a sensible diet and good nutrition first among the foundations of health.

So through today's lesson, we now know which factors are uncontrollable. What can we do for our health? In whose hands is the choice, and who has the final say? We have to put our weight on the part that does 60 percent of the work, our lifestyle, and keep building our own understanding and knowledge of health management.

This period of study is not to tell you that we are very professional. It is to tell you that all of us can become very professional, and to show you a path by which you can keep becoming more professional. That is all I will share today.
