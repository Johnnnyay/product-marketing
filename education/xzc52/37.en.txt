# An Overview of Minerals

Hello, friends. We have now finished the two big groups of protein and vitamins, and today we begin to talk about minerals. What is a mineral? First we need a basic idea of what minerals are. We all know that human beings are a product of the Earth's environment at a certain stage of its evolution. The body is constantly exchanging matter and energy with its environment, so it contains almost every element found in nature. Broadly speaking, there are about twenty or so elements that make up the body's tissues and are necessary for its metabolism and for maintaining its physiological functions.

Among these elements, the first big group is carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen. These four make up organic compounds. What are organic compounds? Carbohydrates, fats, proteins and vitamins, for example, are all made of those four elements. All the other elements are called minerals, and they are the components of inorganic salts. There are dozens of minerals in the human body, and according to how much of each the body contains and how much we need each day, they are divided into two broad groups. Those that make up more than 0.01 percent of body weight, and of which we need more than 100 milligrams a day, are called macrominerals.

These include calcium, phosphorus, sodium, potassium, chlorine, magnesium and sulfur. What are the ones below that threshold called? They are called trace elements. Eight of them, copper, cobalt, chromium, iron, iodine, molybdenum, selenium and zinc, are listed as essential trace elements, indispensable for the body's normal life activities.

So at this point we can see that apart from carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen, all the other elements in our bodies are inorganic minerals, divided into macrominerals and trace minerals. The macrominerals build the body's tissues. Calcium and magnesium, for example, build the skeleton. They also form part of enzymes and can activate enzymes. Calcium, which we all know well, activates ATPase, lipase and protease. That is calcium.

It takes part in our metabolism. ATPase produces energy, lipase breaks down fat, and protease breaks down protein. But the basic material of all of these is still protein. For all of them to work well, calcium has to be involved. So minerals form enzymes, activate enzymes, and maintain the body's acid-base balance.

If the body has more phosphorus and chloride ions, it tends toward acidity, which is what people usually mean when they say someone has an [[acidic constitution|酸性体质, a popular idea in Chinese health culture that a person's body as a whole can lean acidic or alkaline. In mainstream physiology, blood pH is held within a very narrow range by the lungs and kidneys regardless of diet, although diet does change the acidity of the urine.|]]. If there are more calcium, sodium, potassium and magnesium ions, it tends toward alkalinity.

So what is the first role of minerals? To maintain the body's acid-base balance, and to maintain osmotic pressure, which matters enormously to the cell. We know that substances are exchanged across the two sides of the cell membrane. If the osmotic pressure is wrong, that exchange becomes disordered. Minerals are there to keep osmotic pressure normal. That is why the water we drink should not be purified water, because purified water affects the body's osmotic pressure.

That is why I say the water purifier we use should not be a [[reverse osmosis|反渗透, a filtration method that forces water through a very fine membrane and removes most dissolved minerals along with contaminants. The view that drinking it long term disturbs the body's osmotic balance is the speaker's own; for most people the bulk of their mineral intake comes from food rather than water.|]] system, because the water that comes out of reverse osmosis is purified water with no minerals at all. Drink it directly without any minerals, and it will certainly affect your osmotic pressure. Imagine putting a cell into pure water. What happens? The water slowly pushes its way into the cell until the cell bursts. Of course, the body's structure is not that extreme, but when you drink purified water it will certainly affect the body's osmotic pressure. So do not drink purified water straight. Reverse osmosis water can be used for cooking rice and making tea, but not for drinking as it is.

So if you look at what most people use in high-end water purifiers today, what is it? High-density activated carbon. They should not use reverse osmosis water, which used to be water only for industry. When industry makes cosmetics or health supplements, what it needs is sterile, mineral-free water. That is industrial water.

Later, as people began to pay more attention to water quality, some manufacturers brought reverse osmosis water into the home. But is it fine for home use? For cooking rice and making tea, yes. For drinking straight? No. Occasionally is fine, but if you drink it continuously over a long time, it will certainly interfere with your body's osmotic pressure. That is something we need to watch out for ourselves.

That is the role minerals play in maintaining osmotic pressure. Minerals also maintain the excitability of nerve cells. Those are the macrominerals. The trace minerals take part in hormone metabolism and form enzymes, and enzymes in turn carry out the transport of substances in the body.

As for minerals, I think everyone has long been familiar with the idea that the whole nation should be taking calcium. Today we will give an overview of several of the important minerals.

First, minerals share many common features. First, minerals cannot be made in the body. They must be taken in from food and water. And with each day's metabolism, a certain amount is lost every day, excreted through stool, urine, sweat, hair, nails, and the shedding of skin and mucous membranes. So minerals must be replenished from the diet every day. That is the first point.

Second, minerals are distributed very unevenly in the body. Different minerals are found in different amounts in different tissues and organs. Where are calcium and phosphorus mainly found, for example? Mainly in the bones and teeth. [[Iodine|The transcript says 磷 (phosphorus) here, almost certainly a slip of the tongue, since it is iodine that is concentrated in the thyroid gland.|]] is concentrated in the thyroid, and iron is found mainly in red blood cells. Third, minerals can work together, or they can antagonize one another. What is antagonism? It is when two people are working on the same job and get in each other's way, so neither gets anything done efficiently. How should we understand that? Take calcium and phosphorus. If their ratio is wrong, each affects the other's absorption, so that neither calcium nor phosphorus is absorbed well.

Too much copper also inhibits the absorption of iron, and that is also called antagonism. In addition, the gap between a mineral's physiological dose and its toxic dose is fairly narrow. Some trace elements, for example, are needed only in tiny amounts, but the range between the physiological dose and the toxic dose is very narrow. In other words, it is easy to take too much by accident and suffer toxic side effects.

Next I will briefly go through four important minerals. There are so many minerals that I cannot cover them all or go into every one. These four are representative, so let us go through them.

Let us start with calcium. We all know calcium is an important building block of the body. It ranks fifth, after carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen, and it is the most abundant mineral in the body. How much of our body weight does it make up? 1.5 to 2 percent. So how many grams of calcium should a normal adult have in the body? 1,000 to 1,200 grams. What are calcium's physiological functions? First, it is the main component of our bones and teeth. Second, it maintains the excitability of nerves and muscles. Third, it takes part in blood clotting, and it plays a decisive role in regulating cell function, activating enzymes and secreting hormones.

China sets the adult [[AI for calcium at 800 milligrams|AI means adequate intake. In the US, the recommended intake for calcium is 1,000 mg a day for adults 19 to 50 (and in pregnancy and breastfeeding), and 1,200 mg for women over 50 and men over 70. The US upper limit is 2,500 mg a day, and adults over 50 are advised not to exceed 2,000 mg.|https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/calcium/]], 1,200 milligrams for pregnant women and 1,200 milligrams for breastfeeding women. The national upper limit, the UL, is 2,000 milligrams.

Second, let me briefly cover magnesium. Magnesium is the main positive ion inside the body's cells. It activates many enzymes and plays an important role in energy and metabolism. What does magnesium do? First, it takes part in forming bones and teeth, just like calcium, which is why we say calcium and magnesium belong together as one family. Second, it takes part in important enzyme reactions in the body. Third, it maintains the acid-base balance of the body's fluids and the excitability of nerves and muscles. Fourth, it protects the cardiovascular system. Magnesium is also a laxative. What is a laxative? Something that makes you need to move your bowels.

Magnesium ions are absorbed very slowly through the wall of the intestine, so they cause water to be retained. When enough water builds up, you feel the urge to go. So low concentrations of magnesium can ease pressure on the intestinal wall and help the intestine move things along. It also helps relieve cramps.

In China the AI for [[magnesium is set at 330 milligrams|The US recommended intake is 400 to 420 mg a day for men and 310 to 320 mg for women, about 350 to 360 mg in pregnancy. The upper limit of 350 mg a day applies to magnesium from supplements only, and high-dose supplements can cause diarrhea, nausea and cramping.|https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/magnesium/]] for adults and breastfeeding women, and 370 milligrams for pregnant women. Next, a brief word on potassium. Potassium is another of the body's main positive ions and an essential nutrient. It is found mainly inside cells. What does potassium do?

First, it maintains the normal metabolism of carbohydrates and protein. Second, it maintains the responsiveness of nerves and muscles. Third, it maintains the normal function of the heart muscle. Fourth, it can lower blood pressure. Those are potassium's main functions. Potassium's role is especially important in maintaining the electrolyte and osmotic balance between the inside and outside of cells, and the acid-base balance. In China the [[AI for potassium|The US adequate intake for potassium is 3,400 mg a day for men and 2,600 mg for women, and 2,500 to 2,900 mg in pregnancy and breastfeeding depending on age. Higher potassium intake has been shown to lower blood pressure in people with hypertension.|https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/potassium/]] for adults and pregnant women is set at 2,000 milligrams, and for breastfeeding women at 2,400 milligrams. That is potassium.

Next let us talk about iron. Iron is a trace element that people are very easily short of. Iron deficiency is not a problem for China alone. It is widespread in practically every developing country. So where is iron in the body?

Two thirds of it is in our hemoglobin, myoglobin and heme, and the other third is stored as ferritin in the liver, the spleen and the bone marrow. What are iron's main functions? First, it takes part in carrying oxygen through the body and in the respiration of the tissues. You can see how very important that is. I often give this example: why do some children start losing focus in class by ten or eleven in the morning? Because the ability of their hemoglobin to carry oxygen is not as strong as other children's. That has to do with iron, because in the body iron is used to make hemoglobin.

Second, iron maintains normal blood formation. These are iron's most important functions. Iron also catalyzes the conversion of beta-carotene into vitamin A, so you can see it connects to the vitamin A we covered in the last lesson. It also takes part in making purines and collagen and in detoxifying drugs in the liver. Every one of iron's functions is important. Iron also raises immunity by strengthening the ability of neutrophils and phagocytes to engulf invaders, and it improves the body's resistance to infection.

In China the [[AI for iron|The US recommended intake is 8 mg a day for men and for women over 50, 18 mg for women 19 to 50, and 27 mg in pregnancy. The US upper limit is 45 mg a day from age 14. Iron deficiency is the most common nutritional deficiency worldwide.|https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/iron/]] is 12 milligrams for adult men and 20 milligrams for women, so women need more than men. And the UL for iron? 40 milligrams.

I will not spend more time on their other functions, because those are not closely related to our daily lives and health. If you are interested, you can look them up and study them yourself.
