# Probiotics: Our Resident Bacteria and What They Do

Today we begin on probiotics. Probiotics are something the body already has. Our bodies naturally carry a great many bacteria, but as a fetus we have none. When do we get them? As the mother nears delivery, her birth canal becomes covered with beneficial bacteria, so in a natural birth the probiotics are passed on to the next generation along the way. If the baby is born by cesarean section, those probiotics are not passed on. That is the first route by which we receive probiotics.

The second route is breastfeeding. So if a child has had neither, that child's flora will certainly be very thin.

Where in the body are these bacterial communities found? In the mouth, in the respiratory tract, on the skin, in the gut, and in the reproductive tract. We live in a symbiotic relationship with probiotics; we live together with them. Take the mouth. Is there flora in the mouth? Yes. After a night's sleep you wake up with an unpleasant feeling in your mouth and a strong taste. That is because most of the bacteria in the mouth are anaerobic. They do not like oxygen. When you go to sleep, your eyes close and your mouth closes, and there is less oxygen.

With less oxygen, the bacteria grow faster. Anaerobic bacteria multiply quickly when there is no oxygen, so after a night's sleep the level of bacterial growth in your mouth in the morning is very high. The flora is very important. It controls a great deal in our bodies.

We often say the members of a family look alike. Why? Because their flora is very similar. Have you noticed that couples who have been together a long time start to look like each other? That is because their mouths are close together. They are affectionate, they eat together, and their flora becomes more and more alike. So the more loving a couple is, the more they come to resemble each other.

Now you can understand: what we are talking about here is the flora in the mouth. But where in the body is the flora most concentrated? In the gut. Most of our flora lives in the gut. The gut holds on the order of a hundred trillion bacteria, of three kinds: beneficial, neutral, and harmful. These three compete for territory. When the beneficial bacteria are many, the harmful ones are naturally few; when the harmful ones are many, the beneficial ones are naturally few. The neutral bacteria are fence-sitters. They sway back and forth and follow whichever side is larger.

Why do people today so easily have problems with their flora? The first reason is an unhealthy diet, including preservatives in food, and food that is high in fat, low in fiber, or not clean. All of this affects the flora. Then there is the difficulty of adjusting to a new place, what we call not being used to the local water and soil, as well as air pollution and water pollution, all of which affect our gut flora. We also take a great many antibiotics, and surgery, IV drips, and the fatigue and stress of everyday work and study all affect our gut flora too.

Another factor is that our flora changes as we age. The beneficial bacteria become fewer and fewer with age, and the harmful bacteria become more and more. Scientists did an experiment in which they took gut flora from people aged 90 to 120 and analyzed it. What did they find? That their flora was about the same as a young person's. Then they took the flora of people in their fifties and sixties with high blood pressure, diabetes, or cancer and analyzed that. What did they find?

They found these people's flora was in complete disarray, full of mixed and harmful bacteria.
