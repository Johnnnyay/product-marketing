# Calcium Every Day, in Full: Protecting Your Bone Bank

Today we pick up where we left off yesterday. We want to stop the kidneys from casually sending calcium back into places it does not belong, so how do we prevent that from happening? Think about where the whole chain of events starts: the body pulls calcium out, and later that calcium has to be put back somewhere. The source is always the body drawing calcium out of the bones. If you can keep the body from pulling calcium out of your bones in the first place, then everything that follows never happens. So how do you keep the body from casually drawing on the calcium in your bones?

Many of you already have the answer. Take calcium consistently every day, and the body will draw less of it from the bones. The logic is clear, and it is why keeping up with calcium matters so much. It has to be enough, every single day. A lot of people skip it to save trouble, or because they worry about the cost and feel the financial pressure. Having heard this reasoning, they decide they should take it after all. But how should they take it?

Someone asks, what if I take one tablet a day? Is one tablet a day enough? Of course paying some attention to calcium is always better than paying none. But what matters is whether the amount you take is enough to stop the body from freely drawing calcium out of your bones.

So what is the key principle of taking calcium? It comes down to four words in Chinese, which in English means "every day, in full." You take it every day, and every day you take the full amount. That is the principle.

Why do I put it this way? Consider a baby at birth. Is there calcium in a newborn's body? Of course there is. From the moment we are born, the body already holds a calcium savings account, and there is already some money in it. As we grow older, we can deposit into that account every month. Calcium builds up in the bones more and more, and each month the deposit is larger than the month before. So at what age is the monthly deposit at its largest?

Remember this age range: 18 to 20. At that age you might deposit, say, ten or twenty thousand a month. After 18 to 20, the size of each month's deposit slowly goes down. You can no longer put in ten or twenty thousand a month; maybe it is 8,000. A few years later it might be 6,000 a month. And when do the deposits stop altogether?

Remember this age: 35, or around 35. Around the age of 35, calcium stops going into the account. In other words, from birth we can deposit into our bones every month, and the balance keeps growing. At 18 to 20 the monthly deposit reaches its maximum. From 20 onward the monthly deposit slowly falls, and by 35 the deposits stop.

That gives us a conclusion. At what age in a person's life is the amount of calcium in the body at its highest? At 35. The amount of bone calcium the body holds at that point is called [[peak bone mass|骨峰值, the highest amount of bone a person reaches in life. The US National Institutes of Health places the peak somewhat earlier than the speaker does, noting that in most people the amount of bone tissue in the skeleton peaks by the mid to late 20s.|https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/kids/healthy-bones]], the highest bone content you will ever have. The thing to remember is this: the higher a person's peak bone mass, meaning the larger the savings at 35, the lower the chance of osteoporosis in old age. That is easy to understand. The more money you put into your account before 35, the more securely you can spend it when you are old.

Which brings us to the next question. Should you take calcium before 35? Before 35, the body can deposit into the account every month, so do you need to supplement at that age? This is where you have to think it through. At that age you have none of the pain of osteoporosis and none of the discomfort of bone spurs. But should you take calcium? Isn't the answer obvious?

Yes, you should. Why? Because you want to build up a high enough peak bone mass. Put simply, you need to save as hard as you can, because after 35 the body will not take deposits even if you want it to. Then, after 35, when calcium no longer goes into the bones, should you still take calcium? Of course, even more so. Because at that point the purpose of taking calcium is to reduce how much the body takes out of your bones. Put plainly, it is so you do not have to withdraw from your savings every day. If you do not take enough every day, the body has to draw that calcium out of your bones.

For men, after 35 and all the way into old age, the amount of calcium spent each month stays roughly the same. For women it is different.

When a woman reaches menopause, and especially for a period after menopause, the amount of calcium her bones give up each month is quite large. This continues until she is past menopause and reaches about 65, true old age, and only then does her daily withdrawal become about the same as a man's. So every woman goes through a stage of very obvious bone loss, and this deserves women's particular attention.

So after 35, every one of us can only withdraw from the account and spend. Let me ask you: if you dip into your savings every day, how long before you have eaten through everything and the account is empty? How can we avoid touching those savings as much as possible? You have to spend money every day, but you do not want to touch your savings. What do you do? You go out and take on a little extra work to earn some pocket money to cover your daily expenses. Calcium works the same way. Suppose each month you need to spend 800 to 1,000 units of calcium. If you take on a side job that earns you 800 to 1,000 units of calcium a month, you do not need to touch the savings account at all.

If that makes sense, there is one more important point. Say your monthly calcium expense is 1,000, but your side job, meaning your calcium supplement, only brings in 980 a day. You are 20 short of 1,000. In real life you would go to the bank and withdraw 20. But do you withdraw exactly 20? Of course not. At an ATM you would take out at least 100. Nobody goes to the bank to withdraw 20 yuan.

What I am getting at is that the body taking calcium from the bones is a lot like us taking out money. You really only need 20, but at the bank you take out 100. The body is the same. It does not go into the bones and take out exactly the 20 units of calcium you are short. When it withdraws, it may take 100. What happens to the other 80? It gets spent carelessly. What does careless spending mean here? The body finds any gap between the bones and deposits it there, and that is how [[bone spurs form|骨质增生, bony overgrowths also called osteophytes. The speaker's explanation, that calcium withdrawn from bone gets deposited in joint gaps when intake falls short, is his own teaching model. The NIH describes bone spurs as growths on the edges of a joint that develop as osteoarthritis progresses.|https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/osteoarthritis]]. That is why we stress the principle of taking calcium: every day, in full.

Now you can feel for yourself how important "every day, in full" is. It is very important. Only this way can we keep the movement of calcium out of the bones to a minimum, protect the bone mass we have stored, and let our skeleton carry us through a longer stretch of life.

Now someone else says: I know all this. I take calcium every day. I have no bone spurs and no bony overgrowth. I have been taking it for ages, so how am I supposed to feel what it is doing? Some even say: I have taken calcium for more than ten years and I have never noticed any effect. Apart from not having bone spurs, I have not seen any other result.

So let us talk about what the best effect of taking calcium actually is. Here is an example, and it really happened. There was a woman in her sixties. One day she came home, and her children noticed a scrape on her face. When they asked, it turned out she had missed a step going downstairs and tumbled down half a flight. That sounds frightening, doesn't it? After the fall she stood up, brushed off the dust, felt nothing much was wrong, went home, and looked in the mirror. She had scraped her face, and that is how her children found out.

Apart from the scraped face, a few days passed and nothing else turned out to be wrong with her. Her children were relieved and felt their mother was in pretty good health. But I would say that both the children and the mother would find it hard to connect this event with the effect of taking calcium. Why?

Because people in their sixties and seventies fall down steps and break a bone on the spot. This woman fell down the stairs and only scraped her face; she did not feel anything wrong with her body. The whole family focused only on the scrape on her face and forgot that an older person of that age who goes through a fall like that will very likely break a bone. Isn't that exactly the most real effect of taking calcium consistently for a long time?

So what is the best effect? It is that you forget calcium is supposed to have an effect. Think about it: if a young person in their thirties took a fall like that, they would rarely break anything. When a woman in her sixties or seventies gets up afterwards as if nothing happened, just like someone in their thirties, isn't that the effect of taking calcium?

So the most real effect of taking calcium is that you forget it was supposed to have an effect at all. I know that sentence is a little roundabout and I have not phrased it perfectly, but I am sure you understand what I mean.

What matters most, then, is having a healthy mindset. Taking calcium is a piece of knowledge, but more importantly it represents a way of thinking. However good a product is, it is not as important as a healthy mindset. What truly brings each of us health is not only healthy products but a healthy way of thinking. Without that mindset, real health is very hard to achieve.

So we now know that taking calcium matters, and we understand why it has to be every day and in full. How much counts as full? Let us look at the amounts for each age group. For an adult, the baseline is [[800 to 1,000 milligrams a day|These figures follow the Chinese dietary reference intakes the speaker is working from. Recommendations differ by country. In the US the standard recommendation is 1,000 mg a day for women 50 and younger and 1,200 mg for women over 50, while the UK recommends 700 mg a day, and Harvard Health notes that 700 mg from food should be enough for many people.|https://www.health.harvard.edu/staying-healthy/how-much-calcium-do-you-really-need]]. I will leave you some homework: look up the calcium amounts for the different age groups yourself. An adult's baseline is basically 800 to 1,000 mg a day. At menopause it goes up, to 1,200 mg. Pregnant and breastfeeding women need somewhat more again. I am leaving these numbers to you as homework rather than taking up time here. Look them up in the reference tables and it will all be clear at a glance.

While we are here, I want to raise the topic of nutrient intake for breastfeeding mothers. When do you think a woman's nutrient intake is at its highest? For many women it is during the traditional month of rest after giving birth. Once that month is over, many mothers cut back on their nutrients. Is that right? It is certainly not.

We need to understand that for a mother who is breastfeeding, her nutrient intake after giving birth should keep rising, at least until the baby is weaned. Calcium during this stage matters especially, and in the middle and later months of breastfeeding a mother's need for calcium is at its highest. So nutrients should never stop or be reduced once the month of rest is over. They must be increased, because she has entered the breastfeeding period.

Everyone needs to hold on to this idea. It is not that the nutrients come down when the month of rest ends. The mother has entered the breastfeeding period, and she needs even more.

Another topic is calcium for children. Adults often have a misconception here. When deciding how much calcium to give a child, many people compare body size. They think: I take 800 mg a day as an adult, and my child now comes up to my waist, so roughly half should do, and they give the child 400 mg. Is that right? No, it definitely is not. We must never convert a child's calcium amount from body size.

When you look up the tables, you will clearly see that from age 4 to 10, which is from kindergarten through primary school, a child's calcium need is about the same as an adult's. From 1 to 3 years old it is already three quarters of an adult's, which is not a low amount. If we went by body size and gave a three-year-old a quarter or a third of the adult amount, wouldn't that child fall short?

From age 11 to 14, the amount is already similar to what a woman needs at menopause. And in adolescence a child's calcium need goes up further still. If a child is short of calcium, the child can easily develop [[bow legs|O型腿, "O-shaped legs," the medical term for which is genu varum. The speaker's working definition is a gap of more than 0.5 cm between the knees when standing. Some bowing is normal in toddlers; persistent bowing can have several causes, one of which is rickets from a lack of vitamin D or calcium.|]]. What are bow legs? When the gap between the two knees is more than half a centimeter, that is called bow legs.

I remember it from military training in high school, the drills students do at the start of the school year. When we stood at attention, some students could not bring their legs together. The instructor would walk over and try to correct it, but no matter what he did the posture never looked right. Why do bow legs happen? Because that child started running short of calcium early on: short in infancy, short in childhood, never getting enough over a long stretch. By adolescence, it is too late to make it up.

Remember that bow legs cannot wait until adolescence to be fixed. Adolescence cannot repair a child's bow legs, because the cause was laid down in early childhood. On the other side, many middle-aged and older people say they already have bone spurs, so what can they do? To be completely honest, there is no especially good solution. The most important thing is still to take a full amount of calcium every day. If they stop taking calcium and let the bone spurs grow quickly, then when the overgrowth reaches its worst, the only option left will be joint replacement.

An imported artificial joint costs something like 60,000 yuan each. Replacing one does not solve the problem of squatting; both legs have to be done, with surgery on both at the same time. After surgery there may be recovery problems. And it is not as if one replacement lasts a lifetime. An artificial joint has a service life, a number of years, and when that time is up, will you need another one?

So isn't this a painful business? That is why we should first prevent osteoporosis from happening, and then prevent bone spurs from happening. If you already have bone spurs, do not let them grow any bigger, because once bone spurs form they are very hard to reverse.

We see many people whose joints become deformed in old age. Why? Because a lot of bone spurs inside have pushed the joints out of shape. You see bent fingers and misshapen knees, and that is the reason. Some say, I will just keep exercising. But once a person has bone spurs, it is actually very hard to keep exercising. And when a person exercises less, what happens? Their time in the sun gets shorter, because they no longer leave the house much. Less sunlight creates another problem: the body does not make enough vitamin D on its own.

When you do not have enough vitamin D, your body absorbs calcium less efficiently. And it affects something else as well: the chance that your kidneys pull that calcium back also goes down. Put simply, the kidneys stop bothering to pull the calcium back at all.

That is why [[vitamin D|Harvard's School of Public Health notes that vitamin D helps the body absorb and retain calcium and phosphorus, and that the skin makes it from UVB sunlight. At higher latitudes people cannot make enough from the sun for four to six months of the year, so levels often fall by late winter.|https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/vitamin-d/]] is so important to calcium metabolism. While taking calcium, you also need to pay attention to how much time you spend in the sun. In northern China you can wear short sleeves for only three or four months of the year, so alongside your calcium, make sure you get your time in the sun as well.
