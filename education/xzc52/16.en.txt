# Nutrition, Nutrients and Hidden Hunger

Hello, everyone. This is lesson sixteen. Today we will talk about the relationship between the human body and nutrition, and I will also introduce a concept called hidden hunger. Over the past dozen or so lessons we have covered a great many ideas, and I sincerely hope you will listen to them again and again, so that your understanding of those ideas becomes solid. That will help you enormously as you keep learning about health management and about taking in nutrients. Changing your ideas, and making them solid, is the foundation of managing your family's health.

As you put family health management into practice, the difficulties you meet are really collisions with other people's ideas. Only when you have let these ideas shape you can you influence the family members around you. That is why I keep telling you how important ideas are.

In today's lesson I want to help you understand two things. One is nutrition and the other is nutrients. What exactly is nutrition? You will find that different people, at different stages of life, understand nutrition differently. For our grandparents' generation, stewing you a chicken or a duck was what they meant by giving you nutrition.

When I was in high school, my parents started buying me ginseng tonic for the sake of my studies, and that seemed to count as nutrition too. Later many people treated [[cordyceps, bird's nest, highland ginseng and the like|冬虫夏草 (a caterpillar fungus from the Tibetan plateau), 燕窝 (the dried nest of the swiftlet, made from its saliva) and similar costly tonics are prized in China as gifts that build up the body. The speaker's point is that they are popular ideas of "nutrition," not that they supply the essential nutrients.|]] as nutrition. All of this shows that our understanding of nutrition is off. For many people it is very vague. Many people do not know what real nutrition is, and it seems as if there is nutrition everywhere.

In nutrition science, nutrition is a physiological process. It covers eating, digesting, absorbing and using the substances in food that are good for the body. So nutrition is a physiological process with the four steps I just named: eating, digestion, absorption and use. After we eat something, there must be a process of putting it to use.

Say we eat some food. Does it need to be digested? The protein in it has to pass through the stomach and the duodenum, where the intestinal juices turn it into amino acids, and the fat in it becomes fatty acids. After they are absorbed through the small intestine, the amino acids inside the body are built back up into the proteins our own bodies need. So what is that? It is a process of use. Are these four steps important? Very important.

First is eating. Does what you eat matter? It matters a great deal, because it decides how many kinds of nutrients go into your body. It is like driving a car: filling up with 92 octane or 98 octane makes a difference.

Second, can what you eat be digested? Does that matter? Once digested, is it absorbed? Once absorbed, can it be used? If you cannot digest, you cannot absorb. So there is a kind of person whose digestion and absorption are poor, and such people are all small and thin. Chinese medicine calls this a [[weak spleen|脾虚, "spleen deficiency." In traditional Chinese medicine the spleen stands for the whole digestive function of turning food into usable nourishment, not the anatomical organ alone. A weak spleen means poor digestion and absorption.|]]. They simply cannot absorb.

There is another kind of person who can digest and can absorb, so they have a very good appetite, but after absorbing they cannot use it and cannot metabolize it. How does that show? They grow fatter and fatter. So all four of these steps are very important, and together they are what we call nutrition, a complete physiological process. What, then, is a nutrient? A nutrient is a substance in food that, through digestion, absorption and metabolism, can sustain the activities of life. Put simply, only the substances in food that are good for the body are called nutrients. So nutrition and nutrients are different concepts. We often hear people talk about nutrition, and many of them mistake nutrition for nutrients. They are not the same.

We have now discovered more than a hundred substances in food that are useful to the human body. Of those hundred-odd, more than forty cannot be made by the body itself and must be taken in from outside every day. We divide them into seven major classes of nutrients, which is what we mean by nutrients. What are they?

Water, protein, fat, carbohydrate, vitamins, minerals and dietary fiber. These are the seven classes of nutrients. The body cannot make them itself, and they must be taken in from outside every day.

These are the basic nutrients. As science and technology developed, we discovered many more substances that are useful to the body, so nutrients can now also be divided into three broad classes. The first is the macronutrients: protein, fat and carbohydrate. The second is the micronutrients: vitamins and minerals. The third is the other dietary components: water, dietary fiber and [[phytonutrients|植物营养素, also called phytochemicals: the thousands of natural compounds plants make, such as the carotenoids, flavonoids and polyphenols that give fruits and vegetables their color. They are not essential nutrients in the formal sense, but diets rich in them are linked with better health.|]].

Scientists now generally believe that the nutrition in plants is balanced and very useful for the human body. So we extract it from plants, call it phytonutrients, and count it among the nutrients. So what is a nutrient? Only a substance useful to the body is a nutrient. And where are nutrients found? In the food we eat at our three meals every day. Only the substances in it that are useful to the body are nutrients.

Turn that around. If we eat a great deal every day but very little of it is useful to the body, what is that called? That is called being poorly nourished, because nutrients are lacking. Someone once asked me whether taking nutrients, Nutrilite for example, is addictive. Will you become dependent? If you feel low without them and full of energy with them, have you become dependent? Whether something is a dependence depends on whether what you take is useful to your body. Take smoking. Does the body need nicotine? No, but if you do not smoke you have no energy. That is dependence. Take drinking. Does the body need alcohol? No, but if you do not drink you feel uncomfortable. That is dependence.

But if a substance is useful to your body, something your body needs by its very nature, is that dependence? You cannot say you have become dependent on carbohydrate, dependent on rice or on wheat flour, because those are things you must eat. Many of us from northern China love noodles, and if they go two or three days without a bowl of noodles they feel awful. Is that dependence? If you crave noodles every day, is that dependence? It is not dependence, though you could call it a craving.

So why do we never think of eating noodles as an addiction? In fact we are a little hooked, but why do we never count eating noodles as a dependence?

Because it is something we are supposed to eat. Once, something like this actually happened. An elderly woman lodged a complaint against Nutrilite. She said the company's protein powder had made her granddaughter dependent. Her granddaughter was in primary school. Whenever she took the protein powder her grades went up, and whenever she stopped, her grades went down. The woman said your protein powder has made my granddaughter dependent. An expert later explained to her that this was not dependence. Her granddaughter's body simply needed protein. If a primary school pupil's nutrition is sensible, balanced and sufficient, her memory will be good and her understanding strong, and naturally her grades will be good.

If her three meals do not supply enough nutrients, her understanding is poor, her memory is poor, and of course her schoolwork is poor. The expert apparently explained this for a long time, and in the end nobody knows whether the old lady understood, but she bought another tin of protein powder to take home anyway, because her granddaughter's grades mattered. So we need to know what dependence means. Something useful cannot be called dependence, because it is what your body needs by nature. That is the first concept: what the body needs cannot be called dependence. That applies especially to the three classes of nutrients I just described: the macronutrients, the micronutrients, and the third class, the dietary components.

Nutrition research as a whole now pays great attention to that third class, the dietary components, and Nutrilite in particular leads the way, investing a great deal of effort in researching them. The clearest sign of its research strength worldwide is its work on phytonutrients and the related organic compounds within the dietary components, which have a very strong regulating effect on the body. Worldwide knowledge of the macronutrients and micronutrients is already fairly complete, but the dietary components, the phytonutrients, are a frontier of nutritional supplementation still waiting to be developed.

Practice has shown that these phytonutrients have a very strong regulating effect on the body, and around the world new research results keep being added to this broad class of phytonutrients, the dietary components.

Some people ask whether Nutrilite's tea products can be taken over the long term. To answer that, look at it from another angle. Can you drink tea every day for your whole life? The answer is yes, isn't it? Many people drink tea every day. So if the [[tea polyphenols|茶多酚, the family of antioxidant compounds in tea, mainly catechins such as EGCG. The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health reports no safety concerns for green tea as a beverage in adults, but notes uncommon cases of liver injury, mainly with concentrated green tea extracts in tablet or capsule form, so the tea-drinking comparison does not carry over automatically to extracts.|https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/green-tea]] are extracted from tea leaves and made into a nutritional supplement, can you take that for life? Of course you can, can't you? But drugs cannot be taken for life. Some people often ask me whether Nutrilite's functional supplements are only for short-term use. From the tea example you can see that they can be taken long term.

In modern nutrition science, whenever a nutritive substance is classed as a phytonutrient and can be safely extracted, it can itself be eaten as food, so it all falls under what? It falls under the category of health foods.

Let us take protein and carbohydrate as an example. When noon comes, do we get hungry? And when we are hungry, what do we do? We go and eat. Why does the stomach feel hungry? Because the protein, fat and carbohydrate, the macronutrients, have emptied out of your stomach, your blood sugar is low, and you show some symptoms of low blood sugar. Would you run to the hospital and ask the doctor to prescribe something for your trembling hands and racing heart? You would not, because you know that medicine cannot cure hunger. I think everyone understands this. When carbohydrate, fat and protein run short, you feel hungry, and hunger cannot be fixed with medicine. Medicine does not cure hunger.

But there is a class of nutrients, the minerals and vitamins, the micronutrients, which, if they run short in the body and are used up, do not make you feel hungry at all. Yet if they are lacking for a long time, will symptoms appear? They certainly will.

So what does nutrition science call the symptoms of micronutrient deficiency? Remember this term: [[hidden hunger|隐性饥饿. The WHO describes it as the unseen lack of one or more of the micronutrients essential for normal body function, a condition that can exist in people who eat every day. A recent review estimates that about 2 billion people worldwide are at risk of micronutrient deficiency.|https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10960185/]]. We call it hunger at the level of the cell. But do you know when your cells are hungry? You do not. You feel nothing. So why study health management, and why study these nutrients? Because of hidden hunger. Without that knowledge, it is very easy to take the [[sub-health|亚健康, a term widely used in China for a state between health and illness: persistent fatigue, poor sleep, low mood or vague aches with no diagnosable disease.|]] symptoms caused by hungry cells to the hospital. Can a doctor cure your hungry cells? No, can he? There is no drug in the world that can cure being hungry. Everyone can understand this. That is all I will share in today's lesson. See you tomorrow.
