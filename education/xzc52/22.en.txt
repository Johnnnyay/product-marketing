Nutrilite makes nutrients, and as we said earlier, nutrients fall into three broad classes: macronutrients, micronutrients, and the other dietary components.

If we have no macronutrients in our stomach we feel hungry. If we lack micronutrients we do not feel hungry, but the body sends us other signals, and once you have studied, you know what those signals mean, and only then do you know whether the cells inside you are going hungry. Correct?

The vitamins we discuss today are food for the cells. When the cells are hungry they must be fed nutrients, not drugs. That is the concept we keep stressing.

Nutrients cannot replace drugs, and drugs cannot replace nutrients. What is a vitamin? A vitamin is one of a class of organic compounds essential for maintaining the normal activity of life. Apart from vitamin D, none of them can be synthesized in the body, or only in tiny amounts, so they must be taken in from food.

Take vitamin C. The human body cannot make it. We are not like the meat-eating animals, the tiger for example, who never needs to eat greens or fruit. Why? Because a tiger can manufacture vitamin C inside its own body. Our bodies cannot, so we must take in vegetables and fruit.

Except for vitamin D, the body essentially cannot synthesize any vitamin. Vitamin D we can make ourselves, but only with sunlight, with ultraviolet light. As a rule, if you get 15 minutes of sun a day over the three months of summer, the [[vitamin D produced is enough for the whole year|Vitamin D is made in the skin when UVB light acts on a cholesterol precursor, and because it is fat soluble it is stored in body fat. The US RDA is 600 IU (15 mcg) a day for adults through age 70 and 800 IU (20 mcg) after that. The year-long supply from a summer of sun is the speaker's rule of thumb; stores do decline over winter, which is why deficiency is common in northern latitudes.|https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/vitamin-d/]]. Once produced it is stored in the body. Winter does not count. Winter ultraviolet is too weak to produce vitamin D in the body to any real extent, so in summer you must get some sun, and if you did not get enough sun in summer you will certainly be short of vitamin D in winter. As for when to sit in the sun, that is up to you. Nine or ten in the morning is fine. Noon also produces vitamin D, but the ultraviolet is strong. In general, ultraviolet is strong from ten in the morning to four in the afternoon, so before ten in the morning is the best time. The first function of vitamin D is to promote calcium absorption.

Second, it also looks after the rectum. If you are short of vitamin D you are prone to rectal cancer. An important cause of the many rectal cancers we see today is vitamin D deficiency, and if you are interested you can look it up. We say that vitamins do not form part of the body's structure and do not provide energy. What do they do? They mainly drive metabolism. The amount needed is small, but a shortage produces a deficiency disease. What we call deficiency disease, the symptoms of hungry cells, is also called [[hidden hunger|隐性饥饿, the WHO and FAO term for micronutrient deficiency: a shortage of one or more of the vitamins and minerals the body needs, which can exist even when a person eats enough calories every day and feels no hunger.|https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10960185/]]. For example, when we lack vitamin A we get [[night blindness|Night blindness, nyctalopia, is the classic early sign of vitamin A deficiency, because the retina needs vitamin A to make the pigment that lets the eye adjust to dim light. The US RDA is 900 mcg RAE for men and 700 mcg RAE for women.|https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/vitamin-a/]].

When you drive at night and an oncoming car turns on its high beams, everything in front of you goes blank, and only a long time after the car has passed do your eyes recover enough to see the road. That is night blindness.

So when you drive at night, pay attention to yourself. If your eyes take a long time to recover, that tells you your body is short of vitamin A. Everyone should learn a little about vitamin deficiency diseases, because if you have not, you will easily treat a deficiency as an illness, and easily misjudge yourself as sick, when in fact it is a matter of hungry cells. Can a drug treat hungry cells? It cannot solve the problem, can it? So we must know which symptoms belong to vitamin deficiency and hungry cells.

Vitamins are classed by solubility into [[two groups, water-soluble and fat-soluble|Water-soluble vitamins, vitamin C and the B group, must dissolve in water to be absorbed, cannot be stored, and the excess leaves in the urine. Fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K dissolve in fat and accumulate in the body's tissues.|https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/vitamins/]]. The water-soluble ones are vitamin C and the B vitamins. The fat-soluble ones are vitamins A, D, E and K. Water-soluble vitamins dissolve in water, are absorbed through the blood, and are excreted in the urine quite quickly, so as a rule they do not accumulate in the body.

Fat-soluble vitamins dissolve in your fat, are absorbed through the lymphatic system, and are rarely excreted. A small amount leaves through the bile, but most of what you take in is stored in the body. It stays there and does not leave in the urine, so a urine test cannot tell you whether you are deficient, and deficiency appears more slowly. At the same time, they cannot be taken in large doses over a long period, because there can be symptoms of toxicity. So a fat-soluble vitamin does not need to be split into several doses. Once a day, in a full dose, is enough.

Those are some of the differences between water-soluble and fat-soluble vitamins. Let us start with [[vitamin K|Vitamin K is needed to make four of the thirteen proteins involved in blood clotting, which is how it got its name, from the German word for coagulation. Leafy greens are the richest source, gut bacteria make some, and the adult adequate intake is 120 mcg for men and 90 mcg for women.|https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/vitamin-k/]], which mainly manages clotting. When we have a wound, the blood must clot, and vitamin K promotes the liver's production of prothrombin, so it promotes clotting, and it is also called the clotting vitamin. Vitamin K is abundant in our food, especially green vegetables, which are its best source. As long as you eat plenty of vegetables you will not be short of K. Our large intestine also produces some. Sometimes in the hospital, when you are on a drip, they add some vitamin K. In general, people are not deficient.

Now let us look at the main subject today, the B vitamins. The B vitamins are a large family. They include [[B1, B2, B6, B12, pantothenic acid, folic acid, niacin, which is also called vitamin PP, choline, inositol and biotin|The eight vitamins formally recognized as B vitamins are thiamin (B1), riboflavin (B2), niacin (B3, once called vitamin PP for "pellagra preventing"), pantothenic acid (B5), B6, biotin (B7), folate (B9) and B12. Choline and inositol are often sold alongside them in B-complex products but are not classed as vitamins in the US.|https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/vitamins/vitamin-b/]]. We group them as one big family. Why? Because the family shares common features, and their functions are close to one another.

Second, in food they generally occur together as a group. If you are short of B1, that usually means your B2 and B6 are short too. They almost always occur together in the same foods, and are almost always lacking together. They are all coenzymes and all take part in the body's metabolism of carbohydrate, protein and fat. They are all water-soluble and cannot be stored in the body, or only for a very short time, a few hours, so they must be replenished every day. Apart from B12, they all leave in the urine.

That is why we call the whole family the B group. What do the B vitamins do? Most importantly, they have three major functions. The first is regulating metabolism. The carbohydrate, fat and protein we eat must be broken down, and that requires the B vitamins. After absorption they must be re-synthesized, and that requires the B vitamins. The final metabolism to carbon dioxide and water, plus various by-products, requires the B vitamins. The conversion of one substance into another inside the body also requires the B vitamins.

For example, when carbohydrate is to become fat, or fat is to become protein, that conversion needs the B group. And finally, for the body to produce energy, that also needs the B group.

We say that protein, fat and carbohydrate can convert into one another inside the body. Protein and carbohydrate can turn into fat. Plant protein does not turn into fat, which is one reason we recommend plant protein. But for fat and carbohydrate to turn into protein is very difficult. The body burns its energy sources in a fixed order. What goes first?

Blood sugar first. When that is not enough, it starts on fat. Protein is the last line of defense. Under normal conditions the body will not burn protein first. Only a person who has gone many days without eating begins to convert protein into energy, and by then his life may be in danger.

In short, anything we eat that has to be metabolized, broken down or built up needs the B vitamins. So the person who cannot absorb what he eats, whose digestion and absorption are poor, and who ends up small and thin, is short of B vitamins. And the person who absorbs what he eats but cannot metabolize it, and grows fatter the more he eats, is also short of B vitamins. Anything to do with metabolism is related to the B vitamins.

Many teenagers eat a poor breakfast, a lot of carbohydrate and no protein, so after ten in the morning their energy runs out and they cannot concentrate. Once the carbohydrate is digested there is no protein to follow, and that badly affects their studies.

So we use an analogy: firewood, coal briquettes and oil. If you are keeping warm outdoors in winter, which do you burn? Everyone understands the principle. Protein is the premium fuel. In short, everything we eat every day needs the B group to take part, so that what we have eaten can actually be put to use.

If our B vitamins are short, food cannot truly be used, and when it cannot be used all kinds of problems follow. The more food you eat, the more B vitamins you consume. During a child's growth spurt especially, when the appetite surges, the B vitamins must keep pace and be specially supplemented. And the food we eat today carries a lot of toxins, which also use up large amounts of B vitamins, because every one of those toxins has to be metabolized with the B group's help. Today's foods contain a great deal of hormones and antibiotics, and all of that consumes large quantities of B vitamins.

In short, eating any of these things requires the B vitamins to break them down and metabolize them into something useful that the body can absorb, and to break down and clear away the toxins that are of no use. Why do some people seem to carry more toxins? They certainly have fewer B vitamins. Every cell in the body needs the B vitamins. So the person who cannot absorb what he eats, whose digestion and absorption are poor, and who is small and thin, is short of B vitamins.

And the person who absorbs it but cannot metabolize it, and grows fatter the more he eats, is short of B vitamins too. Anything to do with metabolism is related to the B vitamins.

So remember: wherever the absorption and metabolism of substances is involved, the absorption of nutrients and the metabolism of toxins, the first nutrient to reach for, after protein, is the B group. The second function of the B vitamins concerns the building of mucous membranes. It is mainly about the skin. Building skin needs the B vitamins, and the building of inner and outer skin and mucous membrane tissue is all related to the B group. That is the second major function.

Third, anything to do with the nervous system is related to the B vitamins, so the B group is also the first choice for nourishing the nerves. Those are the three major functions of the B vitamins, and all the other functions extend from them: preventing anemia, for example, preventing hardening of the arteries, and preventing neural tube defects.
