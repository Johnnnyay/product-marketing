Let us look at protein. What is protein? Protein is the basic substance of life. Remember this: anything connected with life is connected with protein.

It is the most fundamental material. Without protein there is no life. It is bound up with every form of life, and the most important component of every cell in the body is protein. Apart from water, protein is the most abundant substance in the human body, 16 to 20 percent of body weight. Remove the water and it is roughly 80 percent of what remains. So the body needs protein from top to bottom and from inside to out. But we do not absorb it in the form of protein. The foods we eat must be broken down into amino acids, which are absorbed in the small intestine. The human body uses 22 amino acids. Thirteen we can make ourselves, or convert from other amino acids, and these we call non-essential. But eight cannot be made by an adult, and one more cannot be made by a child, so eight plus one, nine in all.

These [[nine we call essential amino acids|The nine essential amino acids are histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan and valine. Histidine is the "plus one" the speaker refers to, once thought essential only in infancy but now counted as essential for adults as well.|https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/what-should-you-eat/protein/]]. The body cannot make them, and they must come from outside. You can look up the names of the nine in the encyclopedia yourself, and I will not repeat them here.

To judge whether a protein is good or not, we look at how many kinds of amino acid it yields when fully broken down. If all nine are present, and in proportions well suited to human absorption, we call it a complete protein. There is also the partially complete protein, which contains all nine essential amino acids but not in proportions well suited to us.

Finally there is the incomplete protein, which lacks some of the nine. So which foods are complete proteins? Almost all animal foods are complete proteins. Plant foods are mostly partially complete or incomplete. Soy protein, for example, contains all nine amino acids, but the proportions are not ideal for human absorption.

In that sense, animal protein seems better than plant protein, because it has all nine. But turn it around and ask what is wrong with animal protein. That is the basic knowledge of protein. Now, what does protein do? First, it builds the body. It is the main raw material for renewing and repairing our tissues: hair, skin, muscle, bone, internal organs, brain, blood, nerves and the endocrine system are all made of protein. Apart from urine and bile, everything needs protein.

So diet makes the person. Protein is vital to growth and development, and wherever an organ or tissue needs repair, the most basic material must be protein. No Chinese medicine, Western medicine or vitamin can repair tissue. The one thing that repairs tissue is protein. So when protein is lacking, the greatest effect on every organ is that tissue can no longer be properly repaired. This is especially true of brain development. We know that brain development has a particular feature: it happens once. Our brain cells multiply once, and that number is fixed. As a rule it does not increase, and each cell lost is lost.

Human brain cell growth has two peaks. The first is when the fetus is three months old, a peak period of brain cell division. The second is from birth to age one. The first six months especially are a period of intense brain cell growth, and by age one the multiplication of brain cells is essentially complete, with the number already above 90 percent of an adult's.

Whether a person is intelligent depends first on how many brain cells he has, and then on the degree of connection between those cells. Put the two together and you have an intelligent person. So protein intake between birth and age one is extremely important, because it determines how many brain cells there will be. If protein is lacking before age one, the number of brain cells begins to fall, and once the count falls it will certainly affect the child's intellectual development.

Many years ago we had the [[melamine scandal|The 2008 melamine scandal. Melamine, an industrial chemical rich in nitrogen, was added to diluted raw milk so that it would pass protein tests that measure nitrogen. China reported about 294,000 affected children and six deaths, mainly from kidney stones and acute kidney failure in infants who drank the contaminated formula.|https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2799490/]]. In one region, a group of infants of zero to six months were found to have large heads but small brain volume. Their brain cell count had begun to fall. In the end it was found that all of these children had drunk formula from a company that added melamine. Why did the company add melamine?

At the time our national standard required 2.93 grams of protein per 100 milliliters of milk. But the test does not measure protein directly. It measures nitrogen content, nitrogen the element, not protein. The company's protein did not meet the standard, and to pass the test it added melamine, because melamine contains nitrogen. The measured nitrogen came out at standard, but the actual protein was not enough. So this group of infants, zero to six months old, drank the formula, became severely protein deficient, and their brain cell counts fell. With fewer brain cells, their intelligence was lower.

In 2018, the tenth anniversary of the melamine case, reporters went back to investigate. What was the intellectual condition of those infants ten years on? The finding was that it is very hard for them ever to return to normal, because the number of brain cells can no longer increase. Past that age it simply does not increase. The effect on a child's intelligence is lifelong.

What happened in our country after the melamine case? Did the state of milk improve? Before the case, the national standard required 2.93 grams of protein per 100 milliliters. After the case, 2.8 grams per 100 milliliters was enough to pass.
