# How Long Can We Live

Welcome to lesson ten. Some friends have asked me whether they should get a nutritionist qualification certificate by taking a course somewhere. Many of you may have the same question, so let me tell you here: since December 2016, our country has abolished the [[public nutritionist|公共营养师, a vocational qualification that was removed from China's national list of vocational qualifications in late 2016, as part of a broad cutback in state-run occupational certificates.|]] qualification as an official title. That means that the many organizations now running courses that promise you this certificate are, as you can imagine, probably not legitimate, so be careful not to be taken in. What has replaced it? The title of health manager.

Last year, because of the pandemic, although the country pushed forward the health manager certification courses, in Beijing and many other provinces they could not be held.

Starting this year, the country has begun authorizing a number of authoritative institutions to run health manager certification, and those institutions will open health manager courses to the public. So you can all get ready, and when a suitable course run by an authoritative institution comes along, we will recommend it to you as appropriate.

Yesterday we talked about a sense of responsibility and a sense of mission. We hope that in every household and every extended family, the person who leads and makes decisions, above all, understands health. In a family, very often the mother is the one who runs daily life and makes the decisions. The children's health, the grandparents' health, her husband's health are all in her hands.

If the decision maker does not understand health, then when a problem arises, her decisions may go in the wrong direction altogether, and that can bring trouble to the health of family members. The other family members, in turn, need to know how to cooperate. That is why we say that becoming the family health manager in your family is both a responsibility and a mission. So what are we trying to achieve by managing health well? The goal is to live longer, and to live longer in good health. That is the goal. We say three different attitudes lead to three different lifespans. The first kind of person understands health and is willing to live according to its principles. Of course, such people are not the majority.

The second kind is the fatalist. Do we have people like this around us? When someone has no health awareness at all, no matter how you talk to them about health, they will not accept it. These are often fatalists. They say, why talk so much about health, life is for enjoying, eat what you want, drink what you want, and leave the rest to fate. That is one kind of person. Think about how much financial and health risk such a person brings to the whole family around them. And then there is a third kind, of whom there are more and more today: people who believe in the principles of good health but do not know the methods.

In short, more and more people are taking health seriously. That is the trend, because the overall human lifespan keeps getting longer. In slave societies the average human lifespan was only 25 years. In feudal societies it was about 35. Today the average lifespan across the world is already 70. And what is the average lifespan of the Chinese? It is 76.

In the past, human lifespan grew by about one year every hundred years. But with breakthroughs in genetic engineering and bioengineering, from now on the average lifespan will rise faster, with a jump every ten years.

In 1998 the average lifespan in China was 70. By 2019, what was it? [[76|The speaker's figures. Official Chinese data put life expectancy at 70.1 years in 1996 and 77.3 years in 2019, which supports his point that it rose by several years within two decades.|https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9339359/]]. In twenty years, how much did it rise? Six years. How much is that per decade? Three years. So the speed at which average lifespan is rising keeps increasing, which means that overall, humanity will live longer and longer. That is the trend.

The ancients said, [["to reach seventy has always been rare"|人生七十古来稀, a line from the Tang dynasty poet Du Fu, written in the eighth century, which became a common saying about the rarity of long life.|]]. That was more than a thousand years ago. When those words were written, average lifespan was under thirty. Today the average lifespan of the Chinese is already 76. If, with an average lifespan of thirty, people could live beyond seventy, then after a thousand years of development, with average lifespan now starting at seventy, is there not enormous room for human lifespan to grow in the future?

In the Romance of the Three Kingdoms there is a truly formidable figure. Who? [[Sima Yi|司马懿 (179 to 251), a general and statesman of the Wei state during the Three Kingdoms period, whose family went on to found the Jin dynasty. He outlived his great rivals and died at about seventy-two.|]]. Sima Yi was formidable not only in battle but also in how long he lived. When Cao Cao died, he was still alive. When Liu Bei died, he was still alive. When Zhuge Liang died, he was still alive. When Cao Cao's son died, he was still alive. He outlasted all of them, so with his wisdom, dealing with Cao Cao's grandson was easy, was it not?

But in fact he only lived to about seventy. That is why in recent years the World Health Organization [[redrew the age brackets|This classification circulates widely in Chinese media as a WHO standard, but it does not appear in WHO publications. The WHO's own materials generally describe older people as those aged 60 and over.|https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/ageing-and-health]]: 0 to 17 is a minor, 18 to 65 is a young person, 66 to 79 is middle-aged, 80 to 99 is elderly, and 100 and over is long-lived elderly.

By that standard, the friends listening to this course are basically still young people, right? Why redraw the brackets? Because if we did not, as people live longer and longer, would the whole world not become old people? The old standard was 65, and at 65 you were called elderly. But when you reach 65 today, do you actually feel very old? Probably not. People retire at 60 and many still feel young and are rehired to do all kinds of work. So the twenty-first century is the century of longevity, and more and more people will live past 100.

Back in 1998, UN Secretary-General Kofi Annan said that with the continuing advance of science and technology, babies born in 2000 would see the dawn of the twenty-second century.

So since we are interested in health management, and we want to learn it and look after our families well, should we set ourselves a target age? How long do you want to live? How long each person can live has to do with their genes. So how long can human genes let us live? In theory, the age we can reach depends first of all on heredity.

Much scientific research now tells us that inside the nucleus of each cell in the body there are chromosomes, and at the end of each chromosome there is a [[telomere|端粒, the protective cap at the end of a chromosome. Each time a cell divides the telomeres shorten, and when they become too short the cell stops dividing. This built-in limit is called the Hayflick limit; in laboratory studies human fibroblasts stop after roughly 60 to 80 divisions. The step from cell divisions to a human lifespan of 120 years is the speaker's own calculation.|https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5411683/]]. As our cells copy and divide, the telomere gets shorter and shorter, and at a certain point the telomere is gone and the cell can no longer copy itself. At that point the person's life comes to an end. Each round of copying takes two to three years, so how many times do we copy in a lifetime?

Fifty to sixty times. By that standard, how old can we live? Simple multiplication: 120, right?

120 to 150. There is a similar pattern among mammals: [[a mammal's lifespan depends on its growth period|A popular rule of thumb, often traced to the eighteenth-century French naturalist Buffon, that a mammal's maximum lifespan is five to seven times the length of its growth period. It is a heuristic, not an established biological law.|]]. Take the age at which growth and development finish and multiply it by five to seven, and that is the maximum age the mammal can theoretically reach. Humans are mammals too. So at what age does human growth end? It depends on the last organ in the human body to mature. Which one is it? Right, the wisdom teeth. Take the age at which all four of your wisdom teeth have fully come in, multiply by five to seven, and that is roughly the age you can theoretically reach. You can all work out how long you might live.

Someone might say, my wisdom teeth are not all in yet. Between about thirty and forty, the wisdom teeth are all in, so in theory how long could we live? 150. There is a book called [[Homo Deus|未来简史, the Chinese title of Homo Deus: A Brief History of Tomorrow (2016) by the Israeli historian Yuval Noah Harari, which discusses humanity's pursuit of radically extended lifespans.|]] that tells us that with modern technology we can all live beyond 150. Add the progress of genetic engineering and bioengineering, and I think none of us needs to worry too much. One day humans will surely live past 150. Of course, for now we do not need to think about 150. Let us first set ourselves a small goal. How old should we live to? How about past 100? Let us first set the small goal of living past 100.

To be clear, we do not mean lying in bed until 100. We mean living to 100 in health, at the very least able to take care of ourselves. When you really do pass 100, the next goal might be 150. With the development of bioengineering and genetic engineering, it is possible that all of us could live to 120 or even 150.

Look at it from the other direction. If today's chronic non-communicable diseases could be brought under control, would we have the confidence to live past 100? Of course we would, right? Because what takes people's lives today is exactly these chronic non-communicable diseases. Cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease and cancer are what carry you off.

If we take those two away, will our lifespan not grow a great deal? Certainly it will, right? So every one of us should have confidence that we can live past 100. If we remove today's chronic non-communicable diseases, remove these great enemies of humanity, we can absolutely be confident of passing 100. That is our goal, right? And if all of you have that confidence, then the topics we begin tomorrow will become truly meaningful. Tomorrow we will talk about all the factors that affect your health. See you tomorrow.
