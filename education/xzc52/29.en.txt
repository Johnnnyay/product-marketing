# Vitamin C: A Nutrient, Not a Medicine

Starting today we move into a new topic, the antioxidants. Before we begin, I want to remind you of one thing. How do you learn this family health management material well enough to become a capable health manager for your household? I want to give you four key words. The first is mindset: being able to tell drugs and nutrients apart and not confuse the two, and having a real understanding of nutrients. You need to understand why, in the way we live today, we have to supplement nutrients. That is the core mindset. Without it, everything you learn afterward will feel dull and will be of no use.

The second is knowledge: following these lessons to build up a body of knowledge and using that knowledge to empower yourself. In other words, arm yourself with knowledge first.

The third is data. When we pass on a mindset or a piece of knowledge, you need data to support it. The basis for the intake amounts we covered earlier, those figures, you really have to know them by heart. Every point you make must have data behind it. In fact, the books I recommended earlier already contain a great deal of data, and they are a very important source for building understanding of nutrients and for advancing family health management.

This data was not made up on a whim by some company or brand. Take the [[Chinese reference books on dietary nutrient intake|The speaker is referring to China's official dietary reference series, the Dietary Guidelines for Chinese Residents and the Chinese Dietary Reference Intakes published by the Chinese Nutrition Society, which set the recommended and upper intake levels he cites throughout the course.|]], for example. The foundation of that series can be seen as a national health database. I suggest you gradually build the habit of looking up data in these books. Hearing a health theory is one thing. When you can look up the data yourself, the depth of your memory and understanding is completely different. Having data you can put on the table is very important.

The last one is to practice it yourself. Truly make nutrients part of your three meals a day, and use the strength of your own change to influence the other members of your family. In other words, present your own health as a gift to others, so that through you they can feel what health is, something they can see, touch and hold on to. That means taking a big subject, breaking it into small, concrete steps, and doing them solidly and reliably. I believe that is what your family really needs.

Now to today's topic, antioxidants. There are three nutrients connected with antioxidant protection: vitamin C, vitamin A converted from beta-carotene, and vitamin E. All three share a common function, and that function is antioxidation.

Today we begin with vitamin C, and first, how was it discovered? It goes back to [[sixteenth-century Europe|The speaker dates this to the sixteenth century. The best-known episode came later: in 1747 the Scottish naval surgeon James Lind ran a controlled trial on twelve scurvy patients aboard HMS Salisbury and found citrus dramatically effective, though the British Navy did not issue lemon juice routinely until 1795.|]], when ocean exploration had begun. Ships were sailing all over the world and discovering new lands. In those days sailors did not carry any vegetables or fruit on their voyages, so a disease spread among the crews. At first the gums bled, then bleeding slowly appeared under the skin, and in the end the organs throughout the body bled and the person died. This disease was called [[scurvy|坏血病. MedlinePlus defines it as a disease caused by a severe lack of vitamin C in the diet, with weakness, anemia, gum disease, poor wound healing and bleeding beneath the skin. It is rare today.|https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/000355.htm]]. And if you died out at sea, what happened to you?

You were thrown overboard, right? Later some sailors who realized they had scurvy were afraid of being thrown into the sea, so they got off the ship early and went to live on uninhabited islands.

There was not much to eat on those islands, only wild fruit and wild greens, and they found to their surprise that eating these wild fruits and greens cured their scurvy. So at the time they believed that wild fruit and greens could cure scurvy, and afterward ships would carry citrus fruit and lemons on their voyages to prevent it. When people studied these fruits and vegetables, they found a substance in them called ascorbic acid, which could cure scurvy.

But as nutrition science developed, people finally realized that it is not that ascorbic acid cures scurvy. Rather, when the body lacks ascorbic acid, scurvy results. The relationship is not one of treatment; it is one of deficiency. Ascorbic acid was then given a new name: vitamin C. That is where vitamin C comes from. So you should know that people used to think vitamin C cured scurvy, but that reasoning does not hold, because modern nutrition has found that it is a lack of vitamin C that causes scurvy. What is the difference between the two? If vitamin C cures scurvy, that falls under Western medicine, right? What have you turned vitamin C into? A drug, and you are treating scurvy as a disease.

But is it really a disease? It is not. It is a symptom. The problem is a lack of vitamin C. Scurvy is what happens when the cells are short of vitamin C.

What is the root problem? The cells are hungry. I have repeated this way of thinking many times before: it is a problem of hungry cells. There is also a modern saying you may have heard, that [[food and medicine share the same source|药食同源, a traditional Chinese idea that many foods also have medicinal uses and that there is no sharp line between eating well and treating illness.|]]. But looked at now, that idea is not entirely right either, because if food and medicine were the same, wouldn't you start treating certain foods as drugs to cure disease? Can food cure disease? If food appears to cure a disease, what problem has it really solved? It has solved the problem of hungry cells. Then you can understand it.

So we cannot treat many illnesses as diseases. They are symptoms. And what produces the symptom? Your cells are hungry. That is why I say hungry cells are not a disease. When you are short of vitamin C, will scurvy come? It will come for you. If you supplement, will the symptom go away? It will. But if you run short again, will it come back for you? It will come back again.

If we are still stuck on the idea that vitamin C cures scurvy, what stage of understanding is that? Take the mouth ulcers we talked about last lesson. You take vitamin B2, and at the same time you crush a B complex tablet and dab it on the ulcer, and doesn't it heal very quickly? But can you say the B vitamins cured your ulcer? You cannot put it that way, can you? What caused your ulcer? A lack of B2. It is not that B2 cured your ulcer.

Many people are imprecise when they talk about this. They say, "You have an ulcer, take some B2 and it will cure it." If we talk and act that way, it is very easy for others to see the B vitamins as what? As a drug, right? And then someone will certainly feel you are exaggerating, because you have turned a nutrient, a functional food, into a medicine. Other people get very excited whenever protein powder comes up and say it can cure all sorts of diseases. Whenever curing disease comes up, they talk about protein powder. In fact that is often an exaggeration. We must understand that we cannot explain nutrients as drugs. So when someone has an ulcer, what should you tell them?

Why do you have an ulcer? Because you are short of vitamin B2, right? A lack of vitamin B2 is a symptom of hungry cells. So if you supplement B vitamins and there is enough B2 in your body, doesn't the ulcer heal? Will it come back? When you run short again, the ulcer will naturally come back again.

So many people ask, "If I use it, will it relapse?" What does relapse even mean here? This is a problem of hungry cells. How can hunger relapse? If I ate a full meal last night and I am hungry again this morning, is that a relapse of hunger? So we should not use the word relapse, which leans toward the idea of disease. With a symptom, we are talking about what a shortage brings. I am hungry, so what do I do? I eat again in the morning. You cannot say, "I ate dinner tonight, so I shouldn't be hungry tomorrow, and if I am hungry, it has relapsed." There is no such logic, is there?

So what kind of problem is all this? A problem of hungry cells. It cannot be called a relapse. When your cells go hungry, the symptoms in your body come back, don't they? I have said all this to make one point: you cannot take vitamin C as a drug. That is a very important understanding. Vitamin C does have many functions, and it is one of the earliest nutrients humans studied. Where is vitamin C usually found? In vegetables and fruit. From childhood, the adults told us to eat more vegetables and fruit. Why? Because humans are primates, and [[a primate's body cannot make vitamin C on its own|MedlinePlus confirms that the human body is not able to make vitamin C, so it must come from food. The adult RDA is 90 mg a day for men and 75 mg for women, and intakes above 2,000 mg a day are not recommended because they can cause stomach upset, diarrhea and, rarely, kidney stones.|https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/002404.htm]]. It has to be taken in from outside. Most other mammals are different. A tiger, for example, can make vitamin C in its liver, so it never has a deficiency problem. But we humans are different. The human liver cannot make vitamin C, so we have to get it from food.

Someone will say, "All right, I understand your reasoning. But taking your vitamin C and taking pharmacy vitamin C do the same thing. A bottle of vitamin C from the pharmacy might cost a few dozen yuan, and the nutritional supplement might cost two or three hundred. Why should I take the expensive one?"

We would say that the two are of course the same in function. On paper there is not much difference in what they do. So where is the difference? If the raw materials are different, won't the performance be different? A Mercedes-Benz and a [[Xiali|夏利, a small, very cheap Chinese-built hatchback that was once one of the most common taxis in China, used here as the opposite of a luxury car.|]] are both cars, and they do the same job of getting you around. But if you can afford it, will you buy the Mercedes or the Xiali? Why buy the Mercedes? Because the performance is vastly different. The two kinds of vitamin C are described the same way on paper, with no real difference. So where exactly does the difference lie?

Let me give you an example. Here is the first difference. We all eat vegetables and fruit every day. Where do they come from? We pay for them, don't we? Is there a way to get them without spending money? Yes. Every day you could go to the market and pick up the leftover vegetable leaves, and go to the fruit stall and pick up the fruit that is about to rot. Is the vitamin C in those structurally the same as the vitamin C in the vegetables and fruit you paid good money for? It is the same, isn't it? The two kinds of vitamin C have exactly the same structure. Isn't that so?

You could argue that the vitamin C in scavenged fruit and in bought fruit does the same thing, that its structure is the same, and you might even think it is absorbed just as well. So do you scavenge your food or buy it? Why buy it and not pick it up for free? Safety, right?

Safety comes first, doesn't it? Then it is not hard to see where the difference lies. For anything you put in your mouth, safety comes first. Beyond safety, where else is the difference? It is in everything other than the vitamin C. Let me ask you: is there anything toxic in vegetables and fruit that are about to rot? Certainly there is, right? Isn't pharmacy vitamin C the same? Pharmaceutical vitamin C is not something you can eat as food. Pharmacy vitamin C is a drug. What is Nutrilite's vitamin C? It is food grade, it is a food, and so it has to be safe, right? If all you cared about was the vitamin C, you could simply pick up vegetable leaves for free.

But you would not do that. You would pay for your food, because the level of safety is different and the other ingredients are different. That is where the difference lies, and of course the cost is very different too. One costs almost nothing, and the other costs you money. That is a big gap, isn't it? That is the difference we are talking about. If all you care about is the vitamin C, you can say the ingredient is identical. But if you measure it against a food-grade standard, the difference between the two is enormous.

What does food grade mean? Food grade means something you can eat over the long term with no side effects. If you only go out now and then, and all you care about is getting somewhere, you can drive a Xiali or take a taxi.

But if your way of life means driving every day, you need a car with solid performance, don't you? Apply that to life. If you only take it now and then, or you just want to deal with an illness, pharmacy vitamin C will do. But once you realize that vitamin C is not a drug, that it is a nutrient that must be on your table every day, you have to care about its performance, its stability and its safety. Because it is food, it has to be food grade, and it is there to solve the root problem of hungry cells. That is all for today. Tomorrow we will talk about the functions of vitamin C. Thank you, everyone.
