# How to Choose Calcium, and Osteoporosis Versus Bone Spurs

Hello, everyone. Let us continue with the topic of calcium supplements. I think most people today know something about calcium. If you went out today and asked anyone you met whether they know calcium supplements are important, I expect nine times out of ten the answer would be yes, they know calcium matters. So if everyone knows it matters, what should an ordinary person choose to supplement calcium with?

Which company's product should I choose? Faced with so many calcium products on the market, bottles of every color, liquids and solids, and then some that are chelated, faced with this great jumble of products, how do we actually choose? Today we will start to touch on this question.

We need to know that among all the kinds of calcium supplement, [[calcium carbonate|碳酸钙. Harvard Health notes that calcium carbonate contains the most elemental calcium, about 40 percent by weight, needs stomach acid to be absorbed and so is best taken with food, and can cause mild constipation or bloating in some people. Calcium citrate contains only about 21 percent calcium but is absorbed more easily and can be taken on an empty stomach.|https://www.health.harvard.edu/diet-and-nutrition/choosing-a-calcium-supplement]] has the highest calcium content, reaching 40 percent. It does not dissolve in water on its own, but once it reaches the stomach and reacts with stomach acid, it easily becomes calcium that can be absorbed.

What is the advantage of calcium carbonate having the highest content? It means that, for the same size, a calcium carbonate preparation or tablet supplies the most calcium. If you use another form of calcium of the same size, you get less calcium. To get the same amount of calcium, what you swallow has to be bigger before it can give you enough. As for the forms of calcium, there is another one you may have heard of, called chelated calcium. The Chinese character for "chelate" means the two big pincers of a crab. So what is chelation?

Put simply, it means wrapping the calcium inside some other substance. Why wrap the calcium? Because with that outer layer, it gets into cells more easily, and some products use that as a selling point. But when we look at chelated calcium preparations, what do we find? [[Chelated calcium|螯合钙, calcium bound to an organic molecule such as an amino acid. But opening the chelate inside the cell is a technical problem. So we need to understand that chelated calcium does get into the cell more easily, but what gets in easily also gets out easily. This kind of calcium product tends to flow right out of the cell on the other side. The result is that chelated calcium passes straight through, and our cells never get to use the calcium at all. That is the technical difficulty.

There is another situation we often come across in daily life. You are chatting with someone about calcium supplements and you ask, do you take calcium? They say yes. You ask how much they take a day, and they say so many tablets a day. Then you ask how it is working, and you find that their reaction suggests it is not doing much. Their bones still seem thin, they still have bone spurs, and so on. Yet when you ask about the amount of calcium they take, it is enough, not too little.

So here is a question everyone needs to understand. Once we swallow calcium and it is in our stomach, is 100 percent of it absorbed by the intestines? In other words, a person's rate of calcium absorption determines how much calcium they can use.

Some people take quite a lot, but their absorption rate is poor, and the extra calcium leaves with the stool. So what does that mean? Whether what goes into the stomach can be absorbed is already a question; it will never be 100 percent. Then, once it enters the blood and travels to every part of the body, how much is actually used is another question. So there is a question of absorption rate and a question of utilization rate. If a person's utilization of calcium is low, what does that look like? When the blood passes through the kidneys, the calcium is excreted. So a person may seem to be taking plenty of calcium by mouth, but the absorption rate may be low, so a lot of calcium is wasted.

And of the part that does finally get in, the utilization rate is also low, so you find another part wasted. So what is the key advantage of Nutrilite's calcium? Please do not limit it to the idea that this is the calcium with the highest calcium content. We do use calcium carbonate, the form with the highest calcium content, but there is another important advantage. It contains a valuable substance that raises your absorption of calcium and raises your body's use of calcium. What is it?

Another point: ours is not the kind of calcium where one tablet is supposed to equal many. Ours is split into separate tablets. How much calcium does each tablet contain? 216.7 milligrams. What is the benefit of doing it this way? There is a piece of common knowledge about calcium supplements we all need to know. If you take in a large amount of calcium at once, what problem can it cause? It can cause what is called a transient increase in stomach acid secretion. Transient. What does transient mean? Simply put, a burst of stomach acid over a very short time. For many of us, taking a lot of calcium, a large amount of calcium at one time, leads to a one-off surge in stomach acid.

A normal person may not care and may not feel much discomfort. But you need to know that many people already secrete a lot of stomach acid. Even without your calcium, they are prone to acid reflux and heartburn. If you then give them too much calcium at once and it drives up their stomach acid sharply, the transient surge, it will certainly make their stomach discomfort worse.

So how should you take calcium? Calcium must always be taken in [[divided doses|Harvard Health notes that the body cannot absorb more than about 500 mg of calcium at one time, so a larger single dose is partly wasted, and that calcium carbonate is best taken with food.|https://www.health.harvard.edu/diet-and-nutrition/choosing-a-calcium-supplement]], and taking it after a meal is better for absorption. It also avoids the bad effects of calcium and stomach acid meeting head on.

There is one more point. Many people pin the benefits of calcium on calcium itself. They stress how important calcium supplements are and how dangerous calcium deficiency is, and they talk about osteoporosis, bone spurs and all the rest. This is all health knowledge about calcium alone, and here we need to correct that emphasis.

Calcium is of course an important nutrient, but how well calcium is absorbed does not depend on calcium alone. It depends on the other things added with it, such as magnesium, zinc, copper and manganese. So what do magnesium, zinc, copper and manganese actually do for our calcium supplementation, for giving me strong bones?

Let me give an example to help you understand. Suppose we want to build a house. What important material comes to mind? Bricks, right? That is easy. A wall is certainly built up brick by brick. True. But can you build a house with bricks alone? If you are given only bricks, can you build the wall?

Put that way, some people see it and say, no, you can't. I will tell you that actually you can. You can stack them up. But what is the problem? The wall is not solid. Give it a push and it falls down. So if the wall needs to be solid, will bricks alone do? No. What else do you need? Cement and sand. And if the building is very tall, you need steel reinforcement and concrete too, don't you? Supplementing calcium works exactly the same way. If you are given only bricks, only calcium, can it fill out the bones?

Of course it can, but what we want is strong bones. At this point we need to understand that the zinc and magnesium alliance is always building the wall together with calcium. In other words, they work together to build strong bones.

If you are missing the magnesium and zinc alliance, to put it bluntly, your bones will still be brittle. So be sure to remember this point. It is called nutrient synergy, which means the nutrients have to come together as a combination to build strong bones.

Do not underestimate them. Copper, for example. We may not come across copper much in daily life, but if a person is short of copper, they are very prone to osteoporosis. And a lack of zinc can mean the protein in the bones cannot be made. We cannot ignore any of this.

All right, a moment ago we mentioned osteoporosis and bone spurs. Why talk about this? Because many people confuse [[osteoporosis|骨质疏松. MedlinePlus defines it as a disease in which bones become fragile and more likely to break, developing when bone loss outpaces bone formation. It notes that there are no symptoms in the early stages and that many people learn they have it only after a fracture; in advanced disease, compression fractures of the spine can cause pain.|https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/000360.htm]] with [[bone spurs|骨质增生, literally "bone overgrowth." Medically these are osteophytes, outgrowths of bone along the edges of bones where they meet. Harvard Health notes that most are caused by tissue damage from osteoarthritis and many cause no symptoms. The idea that they form from calcium the kidneys have reclaimed, described below, is the speaker's own model.|https://www.health.harvard.edu/bones-and-joints/bone-spurs-overview]]. Let me ask you: when someone has osteoporosis, can they feel it? They can. What do they feel? Bone loss in itself causes bone pain. Where does it hurt? Most commonly in the neck, the shoulder blades, the lower back and the legs.

Take someone whose legs ache. After taking calcium they report back very happily: this calcium is really good, after a week of it my legs don't hurt at all. The neighbor next door hears this and thinks, if it works that well, I'll buy some and take it too. Then you find that for the neighbor it does not work so well. What does the neighbor say?

"After taking it, it seems a little better, but my legs still hurt going up and down the stairs. The pain hasn't clearly improved." So here is a problem. What is it? We need to understand that these two people's bodies are short of calcium in different ways. Once enough calcium is supplied, bone pain caused by bone loss can be stopped, but the pain of a bone spur cannot. To put it plainly, a spur of bone has grown, and a bone spur keeps grinding against the flesh, so that pain does not go away.

So we must tell osteoporosis and bone spurs apart. Both cause this kind of pain, but the mechanisms are different. One is the pain of bone loss. The other is the pain of bone overgrowth, where the bone grows a spur and the spur grinds against the flesh. They are not the same. So two people may both have bone pain caused by a lack of calcium. For the one whose pain stops after taking calcium, the problem is osteoporosis.

For the other, where it seems to ease a little but the pain is never fundamentally resolved, what is the problem? You can conclude it is a problem of [[bone spurs|The transcript says 骨质疏松 (osteoporosis) here, but from the argument the speaker clearly means bone spurs, and the edition follows that meaning.|]]. You see, both are short of calcium, but the mechanisms behind them are different. Which comes first, osteoporosis or bone spurs? It must be osteoporosis. When calcium deficiency continues after that, it gradually develops into bone spurs.

What is the reasoning? We need to know that our body has a mechanism. What mechanism? When bone loss first begins, how is bone lost? Calcium is drawn out of your bones and moved into the blood, and when it is drawn out, it is a whole batch at once. Once that batch of calcium is in the blood, it travels with the blood to the kidneys. One job of the kidneys is filtration of calcium. Here I distinguish two words. One word means filtering in the sense of holding the calcium back. The other means filtering it through, letting the calcium go, so the calcium is filtered straight out.

That is the beginning of our calcium deficiency. You can imagine that if the body did this every day, wouldn't our calcium go down day by day? Before long, what happens when the calcium has all been drained away?

At this point the body has another function, right there in the kidneys. When your calcium is about to be excreted, your body realizes it cannot go on like this, or all the calcium will soon be gone. So the kidneys switch on this function and [[reabsorb the calcium|In mainstream physiology, the kidneys filter calcium from the blood and then reabsorb most of it, a process adjusted by parathyroid hormone and vitamin D to keep blood calcium steady. The idea that reclaimed calcium is deposited at stressed joints as bone spurs is the speaker's own model.|]]. Put plainly, just as it is about to go out the door, it gets pulled back. That creates another problem. Where does the calcium that is pulled back go? Does it go back where it came from? Be sure to remember that it definitely does not go back where it came from. The body has no way of doing that. So where does it go? It goes to the parts of your joints that bear the most load. That is where it fills the calcium in for you.

Say you often bow your head to read. Your neck is under strain, and your body knows that your cervical spine needs reinforcing. So what does it do with the batch of calcium it has picked back up? It pads your cervical spine with it. You could say your body means well, but does meaning well get the job done well? It packs all the reclaimed calcium into your neck, and what happens to your neck? You get bone spurs. Your cervical spine grows bone spurs, and you still move around all the time and keep wearing it down. Won't it hurt?

Or take the knee joint. Your body sees that you are always going up and down stairs, always running, working hard, so it fills calcium back into your knee joint. And doesn't that lead the knee joint to develop what? Bone spurs, right?

So bone spurs do not mean a person has too much calcium in their body, so much that there is nowhere else to put it. Just the opposite. They show that this person's body keeps drawing calcium out of the bones, and in the process of excreting it the body finds that too much is going out, so it pulls it back.

So bone spurs are a sign that a person is losing a great deal of calcium. They show that your body keeps drawing calcium out of the bones to be excreted, which is why the kidneys keep having to pull it back. Now let me ask you: what can we do to stop this from happening?
