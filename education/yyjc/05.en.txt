# How We Gain Weight: Nutrition Basics, Part 3

Where does our daily energy come from? What do the basic nutrients do for us? Does taking supplements regularly create dependence? In this session of the nutrition basics course, we continue with the fundamentals of nutrients.

Next comes carbohydrate. Carbohydrates are what we call sugars, but sugars are not always sweet. How many kinds of sugar are there? A single sugar molecule is a monosaccharide, such as glucose. Two sugar molecules joined together make a disaccharide, such as lactose in milk. Then there are oligosaccharides, fewer than ten sugar molecules joined together, found in things like mushrooms and soybeans; they are also called oligosaccharides. If more than ten sugar molecules are joined together, we call it a polysaccharide. Do we eat polysaccharides every day? Yes, and we cannot do without them. That is starch, the staple foods we eat. Cellulose is also a polysaccharide, only we cannot digest it, though its structure is still a polysaccharide.

First, a quick review of carbohydrate's basic role. Its most important job is providing energy. What share of the body's total energy does carbohydrate supply? Sixty percent.

Now let us look at the nutrient mentioned most in daily life, [[dietary fiber|膳食纤维, the indigestible parts of plant foods that pass through the gut and support digestion and bowel regularity.|https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559033/]]. Dietary fiber splits into soluble and insoluble fiber. Overall it has two main jobs: first, helping you move your bowels, and second, aiding digestion. After eating fiber you can regulate elimination; if you are constipated, more fiber can ease it and make stool easier to pass.

So someone asks, should dietary fiber be taken before or after a meal? Think about it. Fiber is a substance the body does not absorb. Whether you take it before or after a meal, it ends up in the stool. So do not agonize over when to take fiber. If you can, it is best to take more of it whenever possible, because modern people mostly eat refined diets that strip out a lot of fiber, leaving us short. Whether before or after a meal, it helps digestion and absorption, helps you eliminate better, and avoids storing up more retained stool, all of which are good. The retained stool sitting in your gut ferments and breaks down, and the toxins it produces are eventually absorbed by the intestine. When these toxins build up over time and keep being absorbed, a person becomes more prone to intestinal tumors. So we recommend watching your fiber intake every single day, precisely to avoid this problem.

Also, fiber absorbs water, and once in the stomach it swells and increases satiety. If a person needs to control their eating, whether to diet or to control blood sugar, they need to cut back on staple foods, and this is when you want a bit more fiber. With more fiber, it fills the stomach and increases fullness.

Fiber has another trait: it absorbs not only water but also the oil we eat. If a person often eats a high-fat diet, more fiber can bind fat in the intestine and reduce fat absorption, because fiber absorbs both water and oil. If taken together with staple foods, it also lets sugar be absorbed slowly, delaying a rapid rise in blood sugar, which helps the body to a certain degree.

Next is another big class of nutrients, minerals. Minerals divide into two groups, major minerals and trace minerals, and their roles in the body are both similar and distinct. Major minerals are the elements present in larger amounts, such as calcium, magnesium, and phosphorus. They can build part of the body, and the main components of bone are calcium and magnesium. They also have other functions, such as forming and activating certain enzymes, maintaining osmotic pressure, keeping the body's acid-base balance, and maintaining the excitability of nerves and muscles. Minerals present in smaller amounts but still important are the trace minerals. The classic example is iron, an important substance in hemoglobin in the blood. Trace minerals can also make up some hormones, influence nucleic acid metabolism, and help the major minerals like calcium and phosphorus do their jobs. Take copper: if you lack copper, you can even develop osteoporosis, so never ignore the interactions among minerals.

Minerals all share one trait: the body cannot synthesize them, so they must come from food. If you do not eat enough, what follows? Low calcium leads to osteoporosis, low iron leads to anemia, and there can also be high blood pressure and nerve and muscle dysfunction, and in children it can affect growth and development on many fronts. What causes all this? Mineral deficiency.

Another cause is increased loss. If a person has long-standing hemorrhoids, are they prone to iron deficiency? Very much so. In daily life you may not know what blood-loss situations someone else has. There are also women with very irregular cycles, whose irregular cycles cause heavy blood loss, making them more prone to iron deficiency.

There are also interactions among nutrients. People who love strong tea or coffee will find these substances interfering with mineral absorption in the gut.

Then there is increased need. For example, women in mid-to-late pregnancy almost all become short of iron and calcium. If a woman in mid-to-late pregnancy does not take extra iron, she can hardly meet her iron needs and will likely need an iron supplement. You can see how many factors in life can cause mineral deficiency.

Now let us talk about vitamins. Vitamins are nutrients closely tied to the body's metabolism, and they share some traits. First, they are organic compounds essential to the body's life processes, and most cannot be synthesized by the body and must come from food. Second, vitamins do not build body structure. The protein, fat, carbohydrate, and some major minerals we covered can all build body structure, but vitamins do not. Third, vitamins provide no energy and are present in very small amounts. A shortage of any one vitamin causes its own deficiency disease. Low vitamin C causes scurvy, low vitamin B1 causes beriberi, low vitamin B2 causes angular cheilitis, each with its matching disease. In modern nutrition, a shortage of B vitamins is also tied to chronic diseases. So there is a lot of research on how supplementing vitamins generously can help prevent chronic disease, and this is the frontier of vitamin research.

Vitamins fall into two classes: water-soluble and fat-soluble. The water-soluble vitamins closest to daily life are the whole B family and vitamin C. Vitamins A, E, and D, along with vitamin K made in the gut, are the fat-soluble vitamins.

Vitamin deficiency has been common throughout human history, and even today some people still become deficient. The main reasons are three. First, insufficient intake. Some people simply do not like vegetables, and since vegetables and fruit carry the water-soluble vitamins, their intake is often short. Others are strict vegetarians who, for reasons of belief, do not eat animal foods, and they too can easily lack certain nutrients. Second, poor absorption and utilization. If a person's own nutritional status is poor, their absorption and use of vitamins may also be poor. Third, increased need, most commonly in pregnant women and nursing mothers. In China there is a common attitude about nutrient supplementation: take supplements during pregnancy and during the postpartum "sitting the month." Many women's nutrient intake peaks in that first month after giving birth, and after that nutrition gets less attention. Here I want to stress: do not overlook a breastfeeding mother. As nursing continues, her nutrient needs actually keep rising, all the way until the child is weaned.

One more example. What in daily life uses up a lot of vitamins? Staying up late, smoking, drinking, and sweating during exercise. When your daily energy expenditure is high, your need for B vitamins rises. If you burn a lot and do not replenish, you will still show signs of vitamin deficiency. But the process of vitamin deficiency is something people often do not feel. When you are hungry, you know to eat, to take in fat, protein, and carbohydrate; when you are thirsty, you know to drink. Those have sensations. But vitamin deficiency has no sensation, or no clear sensation everyone recognizes. We call it [[hidden hunger|隐性饥饿, a deficiency of micronutrients without obvious symptoms, now a widely used term in global nutrition.|https://www.who.int/health-topics/micronutrients]].

In daily life there are actually many situations where nutrient deficiency shows up; the question is whether you have enough nutrition knowledge to notice it. This is a hidden hunger. So build the habit of a balanced diet, balanced nutrition, and regular nutrient supplementation. It is something needed every day. Do not wait until the deficiency has produced obvious symptoms before you supplement.

We said vitamins split into two classes. Let us start with the water-soluble ones. Water-soluble vitamins share some traits: they dissolve in water and not in fat or fat solvents; they leave the body with sweat or urine; and their storage in the body is small, so they need to be replenished promptly. Most water-soluble vitamins are important coenzymes, especially the B vitamins, which are key coenzymes in energy metabolism. And because their storage is small, a bit of exercise or a rise in energy metabolism brings on a shortage, so deficiency symptoms appear quickly and do not build up in the body.

Fat-soluble vitamins are different. They dissolve in fat and fat solvents, and they are stored mainly in fat tissue, so because they accumulate, deficiency is less likely. But know this: once a fat-soluble vitamin deficiency appears, it means the person has gone a long time without enough. For example, is vitamin D fat-soluble? Yes. If vitamin D deficiency appears, the person has probably been building up to it for a while.

About vitamin D, let me say a bit more, because it is closely tied to daily life and affects everyone's calcium metabolism. For instance, I work mainly in an office. Am I a person prone to vitamin D deficiency? Yes. Why? If I take the subway to work, the moment I step out of the station I am inside the building, so I do not walk far and do not get much sunlight. At lunch I eat inside the building and do not go out, and by the time I leave work it is already dark. In a whole day I may not get more than a few minutes of sunlight. Skin has to be exposed to sunlight to make vitamin D. If I go out dressed like this, even standing in sunlight, which skin can make vitamin D? The face and hands, right? Only that skin can make vitamin D. In a northern winter we may also wear gloves, and gloves alone cut out half the skin that can make vitamin D. Maybe a hat, a mask, even glasses. How much skin do you have exposed to ultraviolet light to make vitamin D? Finally in summer we can wear short sleeves, skirts, and shorts, with enough skin in the sunlight, but many people, afraid of getting dark, cover up. Look at people cycling in summer; some women even wear a little cape to block the UV. And if at this point a person shows calcium deficiency, could vitamin D be a factor? Very possibly. It is not something you solve by taking calcium alone.

Alright, that is our look at the basic nutrients. Thank you.
