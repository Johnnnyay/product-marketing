# What Are We Really Eating? Nutrition Basics, Part 2

Where does our daily energy come from? What do the basic nutrients do for us? Does taking supplements regularly create dependence? In this session of the nutrition basics course, we walk through the fundamentals of nutrients in a systematic way.

Let us begin with protein, and look at what protein actually does in the body and how it moves through us. First, the protein we eat. Notice, is it human protein? No. We are not eating human protein; we are eating the animal and plant protein in our food. Once this protein enters the body, it is broken down in the intestine into individual [[amino acids|氨基酸, the small building-block molecules that proteins are made of. The body links them together into thousands of different proteins.|https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK557845/]], then sent to the liver, where the liver assembles our own human protein for the body's needs.

So what are the main jobs of protein? Remember four of them. The first is building the body's structure. Where do we find protein? Hair, nails, and skin. Is the liver built on a protein skeleton? Is there protein in bone? Of course. The reason you can stand here and be a person, apart from minerals, is that your main framework is protein.

The second job is regulation. Understand that biochemical reactions run in your body every moment. If we ran those reactions in a laboratory, they would need high temperature, high pressure, and special catalysts. In the body, they run at ordinary body temperature and normal air pressure, and they run on enzymes. Where do enzymes come from? If a person lacks protein, the body has less raw material to make enzymes, and various metabolic problems can follow.

The third job is transport. The body moves nutrients around: carrying oxygen takes hemoglobin, carrying fat takes lipoproteins. Many of the "trucks" on the body's transport routes are proteins, and they deliver nutrients to every part of the body.

The fourth job is energy. Can protein provide energy? Yes.

Next let us look at the finer structure of protein, its basic unit, the amino acid. How many amino acids does the body have? Twenty, in two groups. One group is called the [[essential amino acids|必需氨基酸, amino acids the body cannot make itself and must get from food. There are eight (some sources say nine), and they must come from the diet.|https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK557845/]], which the body cannot synthesize and must obtain from food. The body has eight essential amino acids. The other twelve the body can make on its own, so it does not strictly need them from food.

These twelve are the non-essential amino acids. Here is a question: how many kinds of amino acids does a person need to eat each day to meet the day's needs? Three answers for you: eight, twelve, or twenty. Which one? Eight. Because the other twelve can be made from those eight, and together they make twenty. So those eight essential amino acids are extremely important nutrients.

Next is an important concept about amino acids, the [[limiting amino acid|限制性氨基酸, the one amino acid in a food that is in shortest supply relative to need, which caps how much of the food's protein the body can actually use.|https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK234922/]]. Remember one sentence: the closer the ratio of amino acids in a food is to the ratio in the human body, the higher our digestion and absorption of that food. On the other hand, if a food is especially short of one amino acid, even if its other amino acids are high, the unbalanced ratio still means we do not absorb that food well. The missing amino acid is called the limiting amino acid.

A very common example in daily life is natural soybeans. Natural soybeans actually have high ratios of most amino acids, but they lack one called methionine. That is why soybeans, though high in protein, are not absorbed well. When we make soy milk or tofu, the protein is not well absorbed.

Here there is a fix: if we have a way to artificially top up the limiting amino acid, it is like a wooden barrel where we fill in the shortest stave. As the shortest stave rises, the whole food's protein utilization slowly improves. So which food has the highest protein value? There is a technical term here, which, translated into English and reduced to the initials of each word, is [[PDCAAS|蛋白质消化率校正的氨基酸积分 (protein digestibility-corrected amino acid score), a measure of protein quality that corrects for digestibility and amino acid balance, with a score of 1.0 meaning fully usable.|https://www.fao.org/ag/humannutrition/35978-02317b979a686a57aa4593304ffc17f06.pdf]], the protein digestibility-corrected amino acid score.

In other words, a food is corrected by how well humans digest it, and we see what its score is. If a food scores a PDCAAS of one, it means its nutritional value, its absorption and utilization, can reach one hundred percent.

Soy protein isolate has another advantage: its digestibility and absorption are high, and its PDCAAS score reaches one. This means soy protein isolate is almost fully absorbed. Remember this: the higher a protein's absorption rate, the less strain it puts on the liver and kidneys. Which food has the highest-absorption protein? Soy protein isolate.

Now let us turn to fat. First, a quick review of how fat is digested and absorbed, which is a little like protein. Whose fat do we eat? Animal fat, not our own human fat. The fat in the chicken, duck, fish, and meat we eat enters the intestine and is first broken into fatty acid fragments. That is step one, intake. Step two, the absorbed fatty acids travel to the liver, where the liver reassembles them into our own human fat. Once built, this fat is sent out of the liver to every part of the body, under the skin, to the belly, hips, shoulders, and face, to be stored. So remember, fat goes through four steps in the body: intake, absorption, synthesis, and storage. These are the four key steps of fat metabolism.

If someone says they have gained weight and their body fat is high, according to this mechanism it is one of these four steps going wrong: either too much intake, too much absorption in the gut, too much synthesis in the liver, or too much storage. Whichever of the four is excessive, the person is bound to gain weight.

So against this mechanism, if we want someone to slim down, what do we do? Eat less and you may slim; absorb less and you may slim; have the liver synthesize less and you may slim. And even if it is synthesized, there is one last step, store less, and you may slim. So now you understand why we use meal replacements, to reduce intake; and why we use conjugated linoleic acid, green tea extract, dietary fiber, and probiotics to cut fat, because they act on these four steps of fat metabolism.

What does fat do for the body? Its main job is supplying energy. Do you remember how much energy fat provides? Nine thousand calories per gram, which is fat's calorie value. Fat's most important roles are keeping us warm, cushioning and protecting our organs, supplying essential fatty acids, and protecting the fat-soluble vitamins in the body.

Fat divides into two big classes: triglycerides and lipids. Triglycerides are what we call oil, and they split into saturated, monounsaturated, and polyunsaturated fatty acids, plus an unhealthy kind called trans fats. The animal fats we eat, lard, beef tallow, and lamb fat, are mostly saturated. Among the lipids, the health-relevant ones include phospholipids such as [[lecithin|卵磷脂, a phospholipid (phosphatidylcholine) that is a major building block of cell membranes, found in the liver, brain, and nerves.|https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Choline-Consumer/]]. Lecithin is a main lipid on the surface of cell membranes and an important structural component of them, and it matters for the skin, liver, brain, and the health of fat in the blood vessels. There is also a substance everyone has heard of, cholesterol, which belongs to the sterol family of lipids. When you get a blood-lipid test, you are roughly given two numbers, plasma triglycerides and plasma cholesterol, and those are exactly these two classes of fat.

Within fats, today we focus on the polyunsaturated fatty acids. They have a special naming system, and there is one word to remember: omega-3, omega-6, and omega-9. The most important today is [[omega-3|欧米伽3 (Ω-3), a family of polyunsaturated fats found mainly in marine fish oil, linked to heart and brain health.|https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-Consumer/]]. What does it mean? On its molecular structure, a double bond appears at the third carbon atom, so it is called omega-3. If the first double bond appears at the sixth carbon, we call it omega-6; if at the ninth, omega-9. It sounds technical, but in fact the main oils we eat every day are all here. Animal fats are mostly omega-9. The oils Chinese people eat, such as soybean and rapeseed oil, are relatively high in omega-6. And where does omega-3 fat mainly come from?

Mainly from the fish oil of marine fish; freshwater fish contain almost no omega-3. How was this discovered? The Inuit people, who live near the Arctic Circle, can grow almost no vegetables in their environment. Their main food is seal, because they live in extreme cold and need to stay warm, so they eat seal fat. It turned out that their diet is mostly fat, yet their rate of heart and blood-vessel disease is not high. Research found that the seal fat they eat is rich in omega-3. It was through this later research that we discovered how important omega-3 is for protecting human heart and blood-vessel health, and that began the whole line of omega-3 research that followed.

Today we mainly cover two omega-3 polyunsaturated fats. The first is called [[EPA|二十碳五烯酸 (eicosapentaenoic acid), one of the main omega-3 fats in fish oil, best known for supporting heart and blood-vessel health.|https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-Consumer/]], whose full name is eicosapentaenoic acid. What is EPA's biggest benefit? Lowering blood lipids. With enough EPA, it can slow the onset of hardening of the arteries, reduce platelet clumping, and reduce clot formation, which helps prevent heart and blood-vessel disease.

The other is called [[DHA|二十二碳六烯酸 (docosahexaenoic acid), an omega-3 fat concentrated in the brain and retina, important for brain and eye development.|https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-Consumer/]], a word you have heard many times, whose full name is docosahexaenoic acid. Its role is a little broader than EPA's. Beyond lowering platelet clumping, lowering plasma triglycerides, and preventing clots, DHA is also an important substance for making certain materials in the eye and for protecting the health of the brain and nervous system. If you know any mothers of young children, ask them, and they probably know to give their child DHA, because it is a very good nutrient for a child's brain and vision development.

Let me be clear here that fat comes in many kinds. Some fats, like animal fat or omega-9, if eaten in excess can deposit fat in the blood vessels and form atherosclerotic plaque. But omega-3 fats instead reduce the formation of atherosclerotic plaque, reduce clot formation, reduce platelet clumping, and protect blood-vessel health. So we need to learn to tell good fat from bad fat. Deep-sea fish contain almost nothing but omega-3, the good fat, while the plant foods we eat every day contain almost none of this quality fat. Land plants and animals contain almost no omega-3; the richest source of omega-3 is deep-sea salmon.

We also need to know that polyunsaturated fats are easily oxidized by oxygen in the air. What does fish oil look like after oxidation? Natural fish oil is a pale yellow oily liquid, and oxidized fish oil is still a pale yellow oily liquid. You can hardly tell them apart by eye, maybe the oxidized one is slightly thicker. If you cannot tell them apart, let me ask, why do you take fish oil? To lower blood lipids and ease problems in your blood vessels, right? Then what is doing the work? The polyunsaturated fats in the fish oil.

Speaking of fish oil, some people ask, I have heard you should not take fish oil if you have a bleeding tendency, is that true? Let me first ask, what is a bleeding tendency? Here is an example. Suppose a knife cuts my hand, and the hand is bleeding right now. Can I take fish oil? Yes. But I am bleeding, how can I take fish oil? Notice, bleeding and a bleeding tendency are not the same thing. A cut hand is bleeding. What is a bleeding tendency? It is when you are not bleeding yet, but because of a problem in your body, you bleed very easily. For example, [[hemophilia|血友病, an inherited disorder where the blood does not clot normally because a clotting factor is missing.|https://medlineplus.gov/genetics/condition/hemophilia/]], where the body is born missing a clotting factor, so once a wound happens it is hard to stop the bleeding. A bleeding tendency is a rare and special condition, such as hemophilia or primary thrombocytopenia. Because fish oil reduces platelet clumping and prevents clots, for bleeding in certain important organs we do not recommend fish oil, such as bleeding in the brain or the back of the eye. For almost all other situations, fish oil is fine. If my hand is cut and bleeding, I can still take fish oil.

Alright, that is what we have on the basic nutrients. Thank you.
