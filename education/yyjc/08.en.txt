# The Body's Most Basic Building Block: Protein, Part 2

Can a cancer patient eat protein? Can a gout patient eat soy-based protein? How should someone with poor liver or kidney function supplement protein? In this session of the nutrition basics course, the second half of our ten questions on protein clears up the confusion.

Can a person with a tumor eat protein? At first the question sounds odd. Should a cancer patient not be getting more protein? Why the confusion? Dig into the reasoning and you find the thinking is this: I am a tumor patient. If I take protein now, am I not feeding the tumor, and in that way encouraging it to grow? So the worry is whether one may eat protein when one has a tumor.

Let me explain it with an analogy. I am an ordinary person who needs one bowl of rice a day. Now I have a tumor, and the tumor needs half a bowl of rice a day. So how many bowls do I need now? One and a half. Why? One bowl for me, half a bowl for the tumor, right? But if I do not increase my protein and still eat one bowl a day, then who goes short? I am left with half a bowl. Going further, if I now eat only half a bowl, how much does the tumor eat? Still half a bowl, and I eat even less. One step deeper: if I stop eating entirely, not a single bite all day, does the tumor still eat half a bowl? Yes, the tumor still eats its half bowl. If you do not give it that half bowl, it will consume the fat and muscle in your body, and it will still get its half bowl of energy.

So once a person has a tumor, if they do not increase protein, all that happens is they consume themselves to feed the tumor. It is that simple. Please remember, when there is a tumor in the body, we should not only supplement but also help support the tumor's consumption, to slow the body's decline.

This leads to the next question: tumors should get more protein, but does every tumor get more protein? When must a tumor patient not get more protein? The answer is, when liver or kidney function is poor, protein must not be increased. This poor liver or kidney function includes tumors of the liver or kidney, and also non-tumor causes of poor function, such as liver cirrhosis and some forms of nephritis. In all these, protein must not be increased.

But then I ask, must a person with poor liver or kidney function cut off protein entirely? Why not cut it off? Because this patient is still a person, and a person needs protein. What is more, someone with poor liver or kidney function loses a lot of protein in urine, and the liver's ability to make protein is weakened, so they develop low blood protein. They need more, but because their function is poor they cannot have more. So remember, in this situation how should protein be given? One word: control, neither too much nor too little, striking a middle scale.

What is the standard for control? Zero point five to zero point six grams of protein per kilogram of body weight. If I weigh sixty kilograms, how many grams should I control to in a day? Thirty to thirty-six grams. Within that amount, which protein do we choose? Fried potato, fried celery, or braised chicken leg? Remember too, the higher a protein's absorption, the less strain on the liver and kidneys. What is the highest-absorption, one-hundred-percent protein? [[Soy protein isolate|大豆分离蛋白, a purified soy protein with a protein quality score (PDCAAS) of about 1.0, meaning it is very well digested and absorbed.|https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9410752/]]. Protein like this puts the least strain on the liver and kidneys. In the clinic you used to see families, when a patient already had poor liver or kidney function, thinking "let him eat whatever he wants," and they would let the patient have whatever, when in fact that is the time of greatest strain on the liver and kidneys. When you introduce soy protein isolate to the family, because they have not heard of it, they instead think it is something that should not be eaten too much. Let me make this clear: the worse the situation, the more you must remember the word "control," at zero point five to zero point six grams, and when choosing protein, choose the one with the highest absorption.

Now gout. As living standards rise, gout is becoming more common; we used to hear little of it. Gout affects men far more than women, about four to six times more, most often around age forty, and one more thing, bigger, sturdier men are more prone. What is the mechanism?

You know that every food we eat contains a substance called [[purine|嘌呤, a compound found in the cells of all foods; the body breaks it down into uric acid, and too much uric acid can trigger gout.|https://medlineplus.gov/gout.html]]. Purine is basically the stuff on the DNA chain of any food. In a normal person, purine eaten into the body is converted to uric acid, which is then excreted in urine. But a gout patient has a problem: their body still converts purine to uric acid, but the trouble is at the next step, the uric acid cannot be excreted. They have a problem excreting uric acid, and the cause is still unknown, still unstudied, so gout is actually hard to treat.

In a gout patient, uric acid keeps building up in the body, like drying salt by the sea: the water evaporates, the salt gets more and more concentrated, and in the end the salt crystallizes out. The same with uric acid in the body: as it gets more and more concentrated in the blood, at some point it crystallizes into urate crystals. Where do the urate crystals settle first? The big toe, and it is extremely painful. A patient will sometimes tell you, I think I kicked the wall in my sleep last night and hurt my joint, worrying it is a sprain, when actually that is a gout attack.

For a gout patient, converting purine to uric acid is fine, but excreting the uric acid is blocked, and the mechanism is unknown. So to help a gout patient, what dietary advice should I give? Reduce purine intake. With less purine eaten, less uric acid is made, and with less uric acid there is less pressure to excrete it. Right.

Is there any food in nature with no purine? No. Every food is alive, and if it is alive it contains purine. So we can only choose foods lower in purine. First, which foods are very high in purine? Roughly four kinds. The first is beer. Beer is very high in purine, and gout attacks triggered by beer account for over seventy percent of all gout attacks. The second is seafood, which accounts for almost twenty percent. You can see that the common habit of going to the coast and eating seafood puts enormous pressure on someone with uric acid trouble, and very easily triggers a gout attack. The third is seed foods: melon seeds, peanuts, and beans are all seed foods. That is why the dietary advice gout patients get often says "eat less seed food," so can they eat beans? That is the question, and we will explain in a moment. The fourth is meat broth. A gout patient must not drink meat broth, but can eat the meat left after making the broth.

When I say "meat left after the broth," do not mishear it as the scraps at the bottom of the pot. You braise a pot of pork rib soup, the ribs are eaten, the soup is drunk, and what you leave for the gout patient is the dregs at the bottom, that is not health, that is mistreatment. What we mean is the meat left over after the soup is made and the broth removed. That the gout patient can eat. Why? Because purine is water-soluble. The purine in meat dissolves into the broth during cooking, so the meat's purine drops a lot, while the broth's purine rises. So a gout patient cannot drink the broth but can eat the meat. Likewise, seasonings made from meat broth should not be eaten, such as oyster sauce, beef sauce, and dipping sauces concentrated from chicken broth. These are all products of broth, and gout patients should avoid them.

Here a question comes up: we listed beer, seafood, seed foods, and meat broth. The soy-related item is in the seed foods. Natural soybeans we also do not recommend for gout patients. But what we recommend to them now, is it natural soybeans? No. It is soy protein isolate, whose purine content is already very low. But here I want to caution you: because soy protein isolate is something we understand but the gout patient and their doctor may not, the doctor's advice is "do not eat soy products," and that advice is not wrong. So please do not argue with the doctor; understand the reasoning behind the advice.

Now soybean isoflavones. What are soybean isoflavones? Through surveys we found that menopausal women have a series of menopausal symptoms. The mechanism is really the fluctuation of estrogen levels in a menopausal woman's body. Is it low estrogen that causes the problems, or the fluctuation? Actually, entering menopause, a woman's estrogen is in an unstable, declining state. In our country's surveys, what is the main menopausal reaction? Hot flashes. I have not experienced this hot flash myself. It is said that a rush of heat surges up from the chest toward the face, turning the cheeks red, then heavy sweating, which is very distressing.

But surveys found that Japanese women's menopause is not this agonizing. They often get diarrhea, or alternating diarrhea and constipation, so their symptoms are milder than women in other countries. What dietary difference was found? Japanese women are the group with the highest intake of soy products. Research found that the [[soybean isoflavones|大豆异黄酮, plant compounds in soy with a weak estrogen-like effect, studied for easing menopausal symptoms.|https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Soy-Consumer/]] in soybeans have a good effect on the fluctuation of estrogen levels in women.

If a woman's menopausal symptoms come from low estrogen, should we just give her some estrogen? But notice, supplementing estrogen brings a danger: once there is too much, it raises the risk of breast and endometrial cancer. To control menopausal symptoms but end up with breast or endometrial cancer, is that not losing more than you gain? And if her estrogen briefly spikes, we would then need estrogen-suppressing drugs, which gets far too complicated. Think about it: if there were something that could raise estrogen when it is low and lower it when it is high, something that adjusts itself once inside the body, would that not be wonderful? That something is soybean isoflavones, and they have exactly this two-way, protective effect. When your estrogen is high, they help lower it a little; when low, they help raise it a little. Of course the mechanism is not as simple as supplementing estrogen, and it is a much safer mechanism, but in plain language that is what it does, and you can rest assured it is safe.

But here is a problem. To extract isoflavones from natural soybeans, because isoflavones are fat-soluble while soy protein is water-soluble, extracting the fat-soluble isoflavones loses the water-soluble soy protein, and keeping the soy protein in a water-based environment loses the isoflavones. It is hard to have both. I want to tell you that keeping both the original soy protein and the original natural isoflavones in one production process is something only we can do.

Now a common complaint: many people say protein powder does not taste good. You need to know, what determines a food's taste? The higher a food's protein, the worse it tastes. For example, someone says I love meat because I need protein, so I love meat. Now braise them a pot of pure lean meat. Tasty? No, it is too dry and stringy. How do you make it tasty? Pork belly is what is tasty, you need to add fatty meat, and a mix of fat and lean braised together is what tastes good. Notice the problem here: whose aroma is the aroma of braised meat? Is it protein's? No, protein has no aroma. The aroma of braised meat is the aroma of fat. So remember, a food high in fat is bound to taste good. Look at the widely loved snacks everywhere, most are fried starches, french fries, chips, fried dough sticks, fried cakes, fried twists, all fried starch, high in fat and good in taste. Is chocolate delicious? Why? Without cocoa butter, cocoa powder is not tasty. Add cocoa butter and the mouthfeel becomes wonderful, because cocoa butter melts at exactly body temperature, thirty-seven degrees, so it melts in the mouth, not in the hand.

So if you want to improve the taste of a protein food, just lower its protein content. Lower the content and you inevitably add carbohydrate or fat, and the food will taste great, but the protein you get will be low. Why does our food not taste great? Because we want you to get more protein per serving. If you find the taste poor, mix it with milk or other foods, and the taste will improve.

One last question: how much protein should you take in a day? The amount matters. I am an ordinary person. I watch my diet, keep it balanced, get basic exercise, and my work is normal, sometimes walking, sometimes sitting. A person like me takes one scoop of protein a day. Now if someone is going to run a health race today, is one scoop enough for them? No, because they are running, with heavy energy expenditure, and they should clearly take more than I do. So remember, this is an important principle of supplementation, called [[individualized dosing|因人而异, the principle that nutrient needs vary from person to person.|https://ods.od.nih.gov/HealthInformation/dailyvalues.aspx]].

Look again. If today I sit here in class like you, I had one scoop of protein this morning. If today I am lecturing all day, can I still have just one scoop? For the same person, one scoop to listen, but to lecture I should take more. Notice, this is the second principle of supplementation, called timing, taking different amounts at different times.

To sum up, from what protein does in the body, to the points to watch when using protein, to how much to supplement, we have worked through these ten questions. They will not solve one hundred percent of your protein questions, but they will certainly resolve ninety-five percent of them.
